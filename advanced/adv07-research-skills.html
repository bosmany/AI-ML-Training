Module 10.7:
Research Skills for ML Engineers

This chapter assumes you can already build and ship models (Chapters 1-41), track experiments with a registry (mlops-practice/mp02), and run rigorous cross-validation (ml/ch19). It does not re-teach any of that. What it teaches instead is the part almost nobody drills explicitly: how to read a paper fast without getting fooled by its framing, how to design an ablation and a benchmark that would actually survive a skeptical reviewer, and how to tell a real effect from noise before you write it down as a finding. These are judgment skills — every exercise below is still real, runnable code, but the code exists to force the judgment, not the other way around.

7.1 — How to Read a Paper in 20 Minutes, Not Two Hours

Most engineers read a paper the way they'd read a novel: title, abstract, introduction, related work, method, experiments, conclusion, in that order, start to finish. That order is optimized for the AUTHOR's argument, not for YOUR question, which is almost always "does this matter to what I'm doing, and can I trust it?" Related work exists to flatter reviewers and situate the contribution politically — it tells you almost nothing about whether the result is real. Reading it first just burns your first ten minutes on the least informative section in the document.

Read in this order instead: abstract (30 seconds — what do they claim, in one or two sentences, stripped of adjectives), figures (2 minutes — does the shape of the result look like what they claim, or is a "clear win" actually two overlapping error bars), results table (3 minutes — what's the actual baseline, what's the actual number, how big is the gap, and against what compute/data budget), then, only if it's still worth your time, the method section (15+ minutes — how did they actually get there, and could you rebuild it). This order lets you bail after 5 minutes on 80% of papers you skim, which is the whole point — most papers you encounter are NOT relevant to what you're doing, and the fastest way to find the 20% that are is to look at the evidence before the argument.

Read in this order, not top-to-bottom
ABSTRACT ──► FIGURES ──► RESULTS TABLE ──► METHOD (only if still worth it)

"what do       "does the      "how big is the      "how, exactly,

⭐
The results table is where papers get honest whether they mean to or not — numbers don't have adjectives. A paper can claim "substantial improvement" in prose while the table shows a 0.3-point gain with no variance reported. Read the table before you let the prose tell you how to feel about the table.
Check yourself: you have 90 seconds to decide whether a paper is worth reading tonight. What do you look at, and in what order?
Abstract first (what's the claim), then the figures (does the claimed effect look real in the plot), then the results table (how big is the actual gap and against what baseline). Skip the introduction and related work entirely at this stage — they argue for relevance, they don't establish it.
Recap
  • Read abstract → figures → results table → method, not front-to-back.
  • This order lets you correctly bail early on papers that don't matter to you.
  • The results table is the least "argued" part of the paper — read it before the prose frames it for you.
7.2 — Finding the Real Hypothesis Under the Framing

Every paper wants to sound important, which means every abstract is written to maximize perceived significance. Your job as a reader is to separate the hypothesis (a specific, falsifiable claim about why something works) from the framing (the marketing layer around it). "Our approach shows strong performance across a variety of settings and demonstrates the promise of X" is framing — nothing in that sentence could be shown false by any experiment, because "strong" and "a variety" aren't measurable. "We hypothesize that layer-wise learning rate decay reduces catastrophic forgetting because it protects early, general-purpose layers from large updates" is a hypothesis — it names a mechanism, and a result showing early-layer weights changed just as much under LLRD as under uniform LR would falsify it.

The extraction move is mechanical once you look for it: find the sentence that names a mechanism ("because...", "this happens when...", "we hypothesize that...") and treat everything else as scaffolding. Then find the contribution claim separately — what specifically is new here (a method? an analysis? a benchmark? an unexpected empirical finding)? A paper can have a real hypothesis and a weak contribution (the mechanism was already known), or a strong contribution and a vague hypothesis (a genuinely useful benchmark with hand-wavy justification for why it works). Knowing which one you're getting tells you how to use the paper.

Pulling a hypothesis and a "how they test it" claim out of framing
abstract2 = ("Vision transformers trained from scratch on small datasets typically underperform CNNs. " "We hypothesize that this stems from ViT's weaker inductive biases, not from an inherent " "capacity limitation. We test this by comparing a ViT and a CNN of matched parameter count " "on a small dataset, both with and without heavy data augmentation, and observe that the gap " "shrinks substantially when augmentation is added while parameter count is held fixed.") sentences = [s.strip() for s in abstract2.split(". ")] hyp = next(s for s in sentences if "hypothesize" in s) method = next(s for s in sentences if s.startswith("We test this by")) if not method.endswith("."): method += "." print(f"Claimed hypothesis: {hyp}.") print(f"How they test it: {method}")
💡
Notice what's still missing — even a well-stated hypothesis doesn't tell you whether the experiment actually isolates it. "Matched parameter count" is doing a lot of work in that method sentence; if the ViT and CNN differ in anything else (training recipe, augmentation schedule, learning rate), the comparison isn't clean. Section 7.5 is exactly this problem.
Check yourself: an abstract says "our method achieves state-of-the-art results, showing the power of large-scale pretraining." What's the hypothesis here?
There isn't a real one stated. "Achieves state-of-the-art" is a results claim, not a mechanism, and "showing the power of X" is framing, not a falsifiable statement about WHY it works. You'd need to read the method/experiments to find out if there's an actual mechanistic claim underneath (e.g., "more data reduces reliance on task-specific inductive bias") or if the paper is really just reporting a number.
Recap
  • A hypothesis names a mechanism and is falsifiable; framing is unfalsifiable marketing language around it.
  • Extract the hypothesis and the contribution separately — they can be strong or weak independently of each other.
  • A stated hypothesis doesn't guarantee the experiment actually isolates it — check the method against the claim.
7.3 — Reading the Experiments Section Like a Reviewer

A reviewer reading an experiments section is trying to answer three questions the authors would rather you didn't ask too hard: what's the baseline, really — is it the strongest reasonable comparison, or a weak strawman chosen because it loses? What's the ablation actually isolating — does removing one component in the ablation table hold everything else fixed, or did the authors also retune hyperparameters when they removed it (which means the ablation is comparing two different training recipes, not one component)? And, hardest of all: what result, if the authors had gotten it, would have made them abandon the claim — if you can't answer that, the claim isn't falsifiable and the experiments section is theater.

Baselines deserve particular suspicion. A paper claiming "our method beats the baseline by 8 points" is a much weaker claim if the baseline is an outdated architecture the authors didn't tune, versus a strong, well-tuned recent baseline. The giveaway is usually in the appendix or a footnote: was the baseline given the same compute budget, the same amount of hyperparameter search, the same data? If the new method got 50 hyperparameter trials and the baseline got the setting from someone else's three-year-old paper, the 8-point gap tells you almost nothing about the actual contribution.

⚠️
"What would falsify this?" is the single most useful question you can ask of an experiments section — if a paper's claim survives literally any experimental outcome (every result is spun as support), you're not reading science, you're reading advocacy. This doesn't mean the method doesn't work — it means the paper hasn't given you evidence either way.

Keep in mind, too, that the published literature you're reading is itself a biased sample — papers with a clean positive result get published and cited far more often than papers reporting "we tried this obvious idea and it didn't help," even though the second kind of finding is often just as useful to a practitioner deciding what NOT to try next. When you read a stack of papers all claiming variants of the same trick work, you're not necessarily seeing "this trick works reliably" — you may be seeing "this is the kind of trick that gets published when it works, and silently dropped when it doesn't." Section 7.8 covers how to avoid recreating this same bias inside your own project's experiment log.

Check yourself: a paper claims "our regularizer improves generalization" and shows only training curves (no held-out test numbers). What's missing?
Training curves alone can't distinguish "generalizes better" from "underfits the training set on purpose" — a training curve that goes up slower could just mean weaker capacity to fit noise, which the paper is calling generalization. You need held-out numbers (and ideally, a comparison at matched training performance) before "improves generalization" is actually supported.
Recap
  • Check whether the baseline got a fair, comparably-tuned shot — not just whether the new method beat it.
  • A clean ablation holds everything fixed except the one component being removed.
  • Ask what result would have falsified the claim; if nothing could, the section is advocacy, not evidence.
7.4 — What "Reproducing a Result" Actually Means at Small Scale

You will almost never reproduce a real paper's headline result exactly — that typically needs the original compute budget, the original (often unreleased) data, and sometimes undocumented tricks. What you CAN do, and what's actually useful for building research judgment, is reproduce the shape of a well-known, mechanistically-understood effect at toy scale: pick an effect that's small enough to run on a laptop in seconds, specify your hypothesis and method BEFORE you look at the result (Section 7.8 covers why this order matters), then honestly report what you found, including where your toy setup diverges from the real paper's conditions.

This chapter's project does exactly that: a scaled-down version of the classic "regularization reduces overfitting" result (the same shape of finding behind weight decay, L2 penalties, and dropout in real training pipelines) using a tiny polynomial curve-fitting setup you can run end-to-end in the console. The goal isn't to convince anyone this toy result generalizes to a 70B-parameter language model — it's to practice the full loop: hypothesis → method → result → honest limitations, on something small enough that you can't hide behind complexity.

💡
"Small-scale reproduction" is itself a real research skill, not a lesser substitute — a large fraction of real research disagreements (does this trick actually help, or was the original result a fluke of one seed and one dataset?) get resolved by exactly this kind of toy re-run, because toy scale is where you can afford to run enough seeds to know if the effect is even real. Section 7.6 is why that matters.
Check yourself: why is reproducing a SHRUNK version of a result more useful for building judgment than reading ten more papers?
Reading tells you what was claimed; reproducing tells you what's actually true under conditions you fully control and understand. Running the experiment yourself forces you to notice every simplification you made (dataset size, model size, one train/test split vs. many seeds), which is exactly the muscle this whole chapter is trying to build.
Recap
  • You reproduce the SHAPE of a well-understood effect at toy scale, not a paper's exact headline number.
  • Fix your hypothesis and method before you look at the result — this is what "pre-registration" (7.8) means in practice.
  • Toy scale is valuable precisely because it's cheap enough to run many seeds and know if the effect is real.
7.5 — Ablation Study Design: Isolate One Variable at a Time

A clean ablation table has exactly one job: for each component you claim matters, show a version of the full system with ONLY that component removed (or added), everything else held perfectly fixed — same data, same training budget, same hyperparameter search effort. The common mistake is removing a component AND retuning around its absence, which conflates "does this component matter" with "did we get lucky with the retuned hyperparameters." A second common mistake is ablating from the BASELINE upward (adding one component at a time) instead of from the FULL model downward (removing one at a time) — the two can disagree when components interact, and a table with only one direction hides that.

Isolating one variable at a time from the full model
            FULL MODEL (pretrain + augment + dropout) = 0.9000
                              │
        ┌─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐
        ▼                     ▼                     ▼
   -pretrain              -augment               -dropout
   0.8500 (-0.0500)       0.7700 (-0.1300)       0.8700 (-0.0300)

Each branch removes exactly ONE component from the full model, nothing else.
The largest drop names the real driver: augmentation.
A two-component ablation, isolated correctly
def acc(batchnorm, skip): base = 0.74 if batchnorm: base += 0.04 if skip: base += 0.06 if batchnorm and skip: base += 0.015 # small interaction term return round(base, 4) full = acc(True, True) no_bn = acc(False, True) no_skip = acc(True, False) print(f"Full (BN+skip): {full:.4f}") print(f"Remove batchnorm: {no_bn:.4f} (drop {full-no_bn:.4f})") print(f"Remove skip connections: {no_skip:.4f} (drop {full-no_skip:.4f})")
⚠️
The 0.015 interaction term above is the detail most ablation tables silently drop — when components interact, "remove batchnorm" and "remove skip" don't sum to "remove both." A table that reports single-component ablations but claims a combined effect without also showing the both-removed row is quietly assuming no interaction.

Three mistakes show up often enough to name directly. Ablating too many things at once ("no augmentation and no pretraining") tells you the combined effect of both, not either one's individual contribution — you learn less than two clean single-component rows would have told you. Skipping the no-op sanity check — re-running the exact full configuration a second time with a different seed — means you can't tell whether a small ablation "effect" is real or just seed noise (Section 7.6 is the fix). And ablating the wrong direction for the claim you're making: if your claim is "component X is necessary," remove it from the full model; if your claim is "component X alone gets you most of the way," add it to the baseline. Papers sometimes report only the direction that makes the effect look biggest.

Check yourself: an ablation table shows "full model: 91%, no-augmentation: 88%, no-augmentation-and-retuned-LR: 89%." What's wrong with using the last row?
Retuning the learning rate after removing augmentation means the ablation is no longer isolating augmentation alone — you're now also measuring "how much does retuning help," which conflates two changes into one number. The clean version holds LR fixed at the full model's tuned value when you remove augmentation.
Recap
  • Ablate one component at a time from the full model, holding everything else (data, budget, tuning effort) fixed.
  • Removing-and-retuning conflates the component's effect with the retuning's effect.
  • When components interact, single ablations don't sum — report the both-removed row too if you claim a combined effect.
7.6 — Benchmarking Rigorously: Seeds, Variance, Not Cherry-Picking

A single run's accuracy is a point estimate of a random variable, not a fact about the method. Different random seeds change weight initialization, data shuffling, and (for anything using dropout or augmentation) the training trajectory itself — so the SAME method, run twice, gives two different numbers even with nothing else changed. Reporting one seed's result as "the" result is the single most common way research claims turn out not to replicate. The fix is unglamorous and non-negotiable: run multiple seeds (5-10 minimum for small experiments), report the mean AND the spread (standard deviation, or a confidence interval), and never report only your best seed as if it were typical.

More seeds narrow your confidence interval around the same underlying variance — this is why "n=3" and "n=10" can report the same mean but very different amounts of certainty about that mean.

Same variance, more seeds: the CI narrows
import math def ci(vals): n = len(vals); m = sum(vals) / n v = sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in vals) / (n - 1) s = math.sqrt(v); sem = s / math.sqrt(n) return m, sem, (m - 1.96 * sem, m + 1.96 * sem) small = [0.81, 0.76, 0.85] bigger = [0.81, 0.76, 0.85, 0.80, 0.79, 0.83, 0.82, 0.78, 0.80, 0.81] m1, sem1, ci1 = ci(small) m2, sem2, ci2 = ci(bigger) print(f"n=3: mean={m1:.4f} 95% CI width={ci1[1]-ci1[0]:.4f}") print(f"n=10: mean={m2:.4f} 95% CI width={ci2[1]-ci2[0]:.4f}")

Cherry-picking is the other half of this discipline: if you ran 8 configurations and 1 looked great, reporting only that one (without disclosing the other 7) is misleading even if every individual number is true — this is a special case of the multiple-comparisons problem covered fully in 7.7. The rule of thumb: pre-decide which seeds/configs you'll report BEFORE you see results, or report all of them.

Check yourself: two labs report the same method's accuracy as "83%." Lab A ran 1 seed. Lab B ran 10 seeds and reports 83% ± 1.2%. Which claim would you trust more, and why?
Lab B's, even though the point estimate is identical — you know how much that 83% could plausibly vary, and a tight ± tells you the effect is likely stable. Lab A's single number could be an unusually good (or bad) seed and you have no way to tell.
Recap
  • A single seed's result is a point estimate, not a fact — always run multiple seeds for anything with randomness in training.
  • Report mean AND spread (std or CI); more seeds narrow the CI around the same true variance.
  • Reporting only your best config/seed without disclosing the rest is cherry-picking, even if every number shown is true.
7.7 — Noise vs. a Real Effect: Confidence Intervals and the Multiple-Comparisons Trap

Two methods with different mean accuracies are not automatically "different" — the question is whether the gap is bigger than what you'd expect from run-to-run noise alone. A simple, honest check: compute a 95% confidence interval for each method's mean (Section 7.6's machinery) and see whether the intervals overlap. If they overlap substantially, the data does not support confidently claiming one method beats the other, no matter which mean is numerically larger.

Overlapping confidence intervals: higher mean isn't proof
Method A   |------[========*========]------|         mean 0.806
Method B       |------[========*========]------|     mean 0.819
           0.78     0.80     0.82     0.84     0.86
The 95% CI brackets overlap heavily. B's higher mean alone
does NOT show the difference is real -- it's consistent with noise.

The second, sneakier failure mode is the multiple-comparisons trap: if you try 20 hyperparameter combinations against noisy validation data, the BEST of those 20 will look good even if every combination has exactly the same true performance, purely because you're taking the maximum of 20 noisy draws instead of looking at one. The more configurations you try, the more likely SOME of them clears any given bar by chance alone — reporting only the winner, without disclosing how many configurations you searched over, silently launders noise into a "finding."

The multiple-comparisons trap
20 runs of a model with ZERO real hyperparameter effect (true mean = 0.70)

 0.66 0.68 0.69 0.70 0.70 0.71 0.71 0.72 0.72 0.73 0.74 0.71 0.69 ...
                                                    ▲
                                          "our best config: 0.739"
                                     looks impressive alone -- but it's
                                     just the tail of 20 noisy draws
Simulating a null-effect sweep: how good does pure noise look?
def lcg(seed): state = seed while True: state = (1103515245 * state + 12345) % (2 ** 31) yield state / (2 ** 31) gen = lcg(3) # deterministic, no numpy needed null_runs = [round(0.70 + (next(gen) - 0.5) * 0.08, 4) for _ in range(15)] print(f"15 null-effect runs (true effect = 0): max = {max(null_runs):.4f}") print(f"A 'winning' run of 0.712 looks impressive alone, but noise alone reaches {max(null_runs):.4f}")
🚨
The honest fix costs almost nothing: report how many configurations you tried, not just the winner, and — if you can — hold out a SECOND validation set to confirm the winning configuration still wins on data it wasn't selected against. A config chosen from noise usually stops looking special the moment you check it against fresh data.
Check yourself: a report says "config #17 out of 40 beat our baseline by 2.1%, so we recommend it." What question do you ask before believing this?
How big would the BEST of 40 noisy draws look if the true effect were zero? If simulating (or estimating) that noise ceiling gives something close to 2.1%, the "winning" config isn't demonstrated to be better than the other 39 — it's just the one that happened to draw a lucky validation split.
Recap
  • Overlapping confidence intervals mean the data doesn't support a confident "A beats B" claim, regardless of which mean is bigger.
  • Trying many configurations and reporting only the best inflates apparent effects even with zero true effect — the multiple-comparisons trap.
  • Disclose how many configs you tried, and re-validate the winner on fresh, unseen data before trusting it.
7.8 — Research Discipline for Experiment Tracking

mlops-practice/mp02 already taught you how to build the mechanics of experiment tracking — runs, stepped metrics, tie-broken comparisons, a registry. This section assumes that tool exists and asks a different question: what DISCIPLINE do you apply to what you log, when the goal is research rather than shipping a model? Two habits separate rigorous research logging from just-in-case logging.

First, pre-register what you'll measure, before you run the experiment. Write down your hypothesis and your primary metric in the run's notes BEFORE looking at results — not because anyone is checking, but because it's the only reliable defense against post-hoc rationalization (unconsciously deciding, after seeing the numbers, that a DIFFERENT metric was "really" the one that mattered all along). If you find yourself changing your success metric after seeing the result, that result needs a new, pre-registered confirmation run before you trust it.

Second, track negative results with the same care as positive ones. A sweep where 18 of 20 configurations failed to beat the baseline is itself a real, useful finding — it tells the next person (possibly future you) not to waste time in that region of the search space. Silently deleting failed runs recreates the multiple-comparisons trap from 7.7 inside your own project: if only the winners survive in your tracker, anyone reviewing your history sees a record that looks far more consistently positive than the actual search was.

⭐
A pre-registered hypothesis is just a run note you write first, not last — no new tooling required beyond what mp02 already gave you. The discipline is entirely about WHEN you write it, not what you write it with.
Check yourself: after a training run finishes, you notice a metric you weren't tracking as primary looks great. What's the right next step?
Note it as an interesting exploratory finding, then run a NEW, pre-registered confirmation experiment where that metric is the stated primary target before you claim it as a result. Reporting it directly from the run where you noticed it by accident is exactly the post-hoc rationalization this section warns about.
Recap
  • Pre-register your hypothesis and primary metric before running — not to satisfy anyone else, but to defend against fooling yourself.
  • Track negative/failed runs with the same care as wins; a mostly-negative sweep is real information.
  • Silently discarding failed runs recreates the multiple-comparisons trap inside your own experiment history.
Chapter 10.7 Summary
  • Read abstract → figures → results table → method, not front-to-back — this order lets you correctly bail early on papers that don't matter.
  • Separate the hypothesis (falsifiable, names a mechanism) from the framing (unfalsifiable marketing language) in every abstract you read.
  • Read experiments like a reviewer: was the baseline given a fair shot, does the ablation hold everything else fixed, and what result would have falsified the claim?
  • Small-scale reproduction of a well-understood effect's shape is a real, valuable research skill on its own.
  • Clean ablations isolate one component at a time from the full model, without retuning around its absence.
  • Benchmark with multiple seeds, report mean and spread, and never cherry-pick your best run or config.
  • Overlapping confidence intervals mean you can't confidently claim a difference is real; trying many configs and reporting only the best inflates apparent effects even with zero true signal.
  • Pre-register your metric before running, and track negative results — the same discipline that prevents cherry-picking in a paper prevents it in your own experiment log.
