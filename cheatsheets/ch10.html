Ch 10: Calculus & Optimization

Compiled from the chapter text on 2026-09-29. A study aid: the lesson has the explanations and the caveats.

In short

  • The derivative is slope — which way, and how steeply, a function is changing.
  • Gradient descent: repeatedly step in the direction of the negative gradient to minimize a function.
  • Learning rate controls step size — too small is slow, too large can diverge entirely.
  • Partial derivatives generalize this to functions of many variables — exactly what a model with many weights needs.
  • Training a model IS gradient descent on its loss function, with respect to its parameters. You just implemented the core loop.
  • The chain rule (dh/dx = f'(g(x))·g'(x)) is the foundation of backpropagation — applying it repeatedly, output back to input.
  • Loss surfaces can be convex (one minimum, always reachable) or non-convex (multiple minima — where you start can change where you end up).
  • Batch/SGD/mini-batch trade off gradient quality vs. cost per step; momentum/Adam add memory across steps to handle ill-conditioned surfaces.

Key ideas by section

The Derivative: Which Way Is Downhill?

  • The derivative of a function at a point tells you its slope there — how fast it's changing, and in which direction.
  • That's the entire intuition you need: the derivative points uphill; the negative derivative points downhill.
  • If you're standing on a curve and the derivative is positive, going right increases the function's value; going left decreases it.

Computing a Derivative Numerically

  • A numerical derivative approximates the slope by checking the function's value at two nearby points and dividing by the distance between them — a direct translation of "rise over run."
  • You don't need to remember calculus rules to use derivatives in code.

Gradient Descent: The Algorithm That Trains Every Model

  • This is gradient descent: the algorithm underneath linear regression, logistic regression, and every neural network you will ever train.
  • To MINIMIZE a function, repeatedly step in the direction of the NEGATIVE derivative — downhill.

Learning Rate: The Most Important Number in ML Training

  • The learning rate controls step size.
  • Too large, and you overshoot the minimum — sometimes so badly the loss explodes instead of shrinking.

Partial Derivatives: Gradients in Multiple Dimensions

  • A partial derivative is the slope with respect to ONE variable, holding the others fixed.
  • The gradient is the vector of all partial derivatives — it points in the direction of steepest increase, across every dimension at once.
  • Real models have many parameters, not just one.

The Chain Rule and the Foundation of Backpropagation

  • Real functions are built by composing simpler ones: h(x) = f(g(x)).
  • The chain rule says the derivative of a composition is the product of the pieces' derivatives: dh/dx = f'(g(x)) · g'(x) — differentiate the outer function (evaluated at the inner function's output), then multiply by the inner function's own derivative.

Loss Functions and Loss Surfaces

  • Every "function" this chapter minimized was secretly a loss function — a single number measuring how wrong a model's parameters currently are (smaller is better).
  • Plot a loss function's value across every combination of its parameters, and you get a loss surface — the literal landscape gradient descent is walking across.
  • Chapter 9's MSE and Chapter 16's log-loss are both loss functions; this chapter's (x-3)² toy example is a loss function too, just for a model with one made-up parameter instead of real weights.

Convex vs Non-Convex Optimization

  • The bowl above is convex: it has exactly one minimum, and every "locally lowest point" IS the global lowest point — gradient descent can't get trapped.
  • Most neural network loss surfaces are non-convex: many bumps, valleys, and saddle points, where a local minimum is NOT guaranteed to be the best possible minimum.
  • Linear and logistic regression's loss surfaces are always convex like this.

  • Plain gradient descent treats every step independently — it has no memory. Momentum adds a "velocity" that accumulates gradients over time (v = β·v + gradient, then param -= lr·v): directions the gradient keeps pointing…
  • This helps most on ill-conditioned loss surfaces — steep in one direction, shallow in another — which plain gradient descent handles poorly no matter what single learning rate you pick.

Watch out

  • If your model's loss is increasing or oscillating wildly during training, the learning rate is almost always the first thing to check. This single number causes more failed training runs than any other hyperparameter.
