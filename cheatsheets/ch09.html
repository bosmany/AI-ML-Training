Ch 9: Probability & Statistics

Compiled from the chapter text on 2026-09-29. A study aid: the lesson has the explanations and the caveats.

In short

  • Mean is sensitive to outliers; median isn't; mode is the most frequent value.
  • Variance/std dev measure spread; expectation E[X] and Var(X) are their theoretical counterparts. Z-score = (x - mean) / std — how unusual a value is.
  • Distributions — Bernoulli/Binomial (labels), Poisson (counts), Uniform (random init), Normal (noise, CLT) — model different shapes of ML data.
  • Correlation (np.corrcoef) ranges -1 to 1. It is NOT causation — a hidden confounder can create strong correlation with zero direct link.
  • Conditional probability P(A|B) is generally different from plain P(A).
  • Bayes' theorem flips P(evidence|truth) into P(truth|evidence) — powers rare-event detection AND naive-Bayes spam classification.
  • The CLT: sample means become approximately normal as n grows, with spread shrinking as 1/√n — regardless of the population's own shape.
  • P-values, confidence intervals and A/B tests quantify "is this real or noise?" — but peeking early inflates false positives, and neither a p-value nor a CI means what people often assume.

Key ideas by section

Why Statistics Matters in ML

  • Before you train a model, you need to describe your data: what's typical, how spread out it is, whether two features move together.
  • After you train a model, you need to describe how well it did — and whether that's actually good, or just luck.
  • Statistics is the toolkit for both.

Mean, Median, and Mode

  • The mean (average) is sensitive to outliers — one huge value can drag it far from what's "typical." The median (the middle value once sorted) ignores outliers entirely.
  • The mode is simply the most frequent value.

Variance and Standard Deviation: Measuring Spread

  • np.mean and np.var compute sample statistics — estimates from data you actually collected.
  • Two datasets can have the exact same mean and look completely different. Variance measures how spread out values are from the mean (average of squared deviations). Standard deviation is just the square root of variance — back in…
  • When you don't know the true distribution — the usual case — the sample mean and sample variance are your best estimates of E[X] and Var(X).

Probability Distributions in ML

  • A z-score converts any value into "how many standard deviations from the mean" — z = (x - mean) / std — which is exactly how you flag anomalies.
  • A probability distribution describes every possible outcome of some random process and how likely each one is.
  • The normal distribution (bell curve) is worth its own spotlight — it's the one you'll use constantly for continuous data.

Conditional Probability

  • P(A | B) reads "the probability of A, given that B happened" — a DIFFERENT number from the plain probability of A. This is the basis of every classifier's confidence score.

Bayes' Theorem: Updating Beliefs With Evidence

  • Bayes' theorem is also the direct ancestor of a real, classic ML algorithm: Naive Bayes spam classification.
  • Bayes' theorem flips a conditional probability around: it turns "P(evidence | truth)" — which is usually what you can measure — into "P(truth | evidence)" — which is usually what you actually want to know.
  • The idea: start with a prior belief that any given email is spam, then update that belief using evidence — words in the email — via Bayes' theorem.

Sampling and the Central Limit Theorem

  • You almost never see the whole population — you see a sample, and use it to estimate the population's true mean.

Hypothesis Testing, P-Values and Confidence Intervals

  • A confidence interval (CI) gives you a range instead of a single point estimate: mean ± z · (std / √n), where z ≈ 1.96 for 95% confidence (that 1.96 is exactly the z-score from 9.4's normal-distribution table).
  • The p-value answers exactly that: the probability of seeing a difference at least this large, purely by chance, if the null hypothesis were true.
  • A hypothesis test starts by assuming nothing interesting is happening — the null hypothesis (H₀, e.g. "these two model variants perform equally well") — then asks: if that were true, how surprising is the data I actually observed?

A/B Testing in Practice

  • With two proportions, the standard tool is a two-proportion z-test.
  • A/B testing is applied hypothesis testing: show variant A to one group of users, variant B to another, and use a test to decide whether an observed difference in conversion rate is real or just noise.
