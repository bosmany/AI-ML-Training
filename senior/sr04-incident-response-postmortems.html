Senior Engineering 4:
Incidents, Postmortems & Code Review

Systems fail. What separates a senior engineer is not that their code never breaks, it is what happens in the first ten minutes afterwards and in the week that follows. This chapter teaches the whole loop: how to decide how bad something is, how to stop the bleeding before you understand it, how to debug with a method instead of a hunch, how to write a postmortem that changes the system rather than blaming a person, and how to review code so fewer incidents happen at all. Almost every idea has a small runnable exercise on realistic, made-up logs, metrics and diffs.

4.1 — How Bad Is It? Severity, SLIs, SLOs and Error Budgets

Meet the running example for this whole chapter: Pebble Market, a small online shop. Its most important feature is POST /checkout, the request that turns a basket into an order. On a Tuesday afternoon Asha, who is on call, sees two messages at once. One says the footer of the About page has a typo. The other says some customers get an error page when they pay. Both messages say "urgent". Which one do you drop everything for? Without a shared way to answer, teams fail in two directions: they wake six people at 3 a.m. for a typo, or they let a payment failure sit until Monday because nobody was sure it counted.

The fix is to agree on vocabulary before the emergency. Three terms do most of the work. An SLI (service level indicator) is a measurement of how well the service is doing from the user's side, usually a ratio: good events divided by valid events. "Checkouts that returned a non-error response within 800 ms, divided by all checkouts" is an SLI. An SLO (service level objective) is the target you choose for that indicator over a window of time, for example "99.9% of checkouts are good over 30 days". An SLA (service level agreement) is a promise to a customer with consequences attached, such as a refund. Think of driving: the SLI is your speedometer reading, the SLO is the speed you told yourself you would keep, and the SLA is the ticket you get if you break the posted limit. Engineers set SLOs; lawyers and sales teams set SLAs, and the SLA should always be looser than the SLO so you notice trouble before you owe money.

The error budget: permission to fail, in numbers

If the SLO is 99.9%, then 0.1% of events are allowed to be bad. That allowance is the error budget. It turns a vague wish ("be reliable") into a number you can spend. Shipping a risky feature spends budget if it breaks something. A quiet month leaves budget unspent, which is a sign you could ship faster. Why not aim for 100%? Because each extra nine costs roughly ten times more, users cannot tell the difference once their own phone and network fail more often than you do, and a service that never changes is a service that never improves. The first block turns the percentage into minutes so the numbers feel real.

Allowed downtime per 30 days for each target
MINUTES = 30 * 24 * 60 # 43,200 minutes in a 30-day window for slo in (99.0, 99.5, 99.9, 99.95, 99.99): budget_fraction = 1 - slo / 100 minutes = MINUTES * budget_fraction print(f"SLO {slo:>5}% -> budget {budget_fraction:.4%} -> {minutes:7.1f} min of full outage")
SLO 99.0% -> budget 1.0000% -> 432.0 min of full outage SLO 99.5% -> budget 0.5000% -> 216.0 min of full outage SLO 99.9% -> budget 0.1000% -> 43.2 min of full outage SLO 99.95% -> budget 0.0500% -> 21.6 min of full outage SLO 99.99% -> budget 0.0100% -> 4.3 min of full outage

What Python does, line by line. (1) MINUTES is the length of the window in minutes. (2) The loop visits five candidate targets. (3) 1 - slo/100 converts a percentage into the fraction that is allowed to fail. (4) Multiplying by the window gives minutes. Notice that going from 99.9% to 99.99% shrinks the budget from 43.2 minutes to 4.3 minutes: less time than a typical deploy takes to roll back. That is why the fourth nine is a serious engineering commitment, not a rounding choice.

Spending the budget: consumption and burn rate

Time is only one way to count. Most services measure requests instead, because a one-minute outage at 3 a.m. hurts far fewer people than one at lunch. Suppose Pebble Market served 10,000,000 checkout requests this month and the SLO is 99.9%. The budget is 10,000 bad requests. If 4,200 have failed so far, 42% of the budget is gone. The number that matters most during an incident is the burn rate: how fast you are spending relative to the rate that would exactly use up the budget at the end of the window. A burn rate of 1 means you will land on zero at the deadline. A burn rate of 10 means the whole month's budget is gone in three days. Burn rate is what alerts should watch, because it says both "something is wrong" and "how urgent it is" in one number.

Consumption and burn rate
def budget_report(slo, window_requests, bad, window_h, elapsed_h): allowed = window_requests * (1 - slo) # bad requests the whole window may contain consumed = bad / allowed # share of the budget already spent burn = consumed / (elapsed_h / window_h) # 1.0 == spending exactly on plan hours_left = (1 - consumed) / (consumed / elapsed_h) return allowed, consumed, burn, hours_left # One hour of a bad deploy: 300 failures at 50,000 requests/hour (36M in a 720 h month) allowed, consumed, burn, left = budget_report(0.999, 36_000_000, 300, 720, 1) print(f"one bad hour: {consumed:.1%} of the month's budget, burn rate {burn:.1f}x") # Whole month is 10M requests; 4,200 bad so far, 240 hours in allowed, consumed, burn, left = budget_report(0.999, 10_000_000, 4200, 720, 240) print(f"month so far: {consumed:.0%} consumed, burn {burn:.2f}x, empty in ~{left:.0f} more hours")
one bad hour: 0.8% of the month's budget, burn rate 6.0x month so far: 42% consumed, burn 1.26x, empty in ~331 more hours

Read the first line of output carefully: 300 failures in an hour looks small, but it burns 0.8% of the whole month's budget in one hour, which is six times the pace the plan allows. In the second scenario one third of the month has passed and 42% of the budget is gone, a burn rate of 1.26x: at this pace the budget runs out in about 331 more hours, before the 720-hour window closes. Numbers like these are what let a team say "we should stop shipping features and fix reliability" without a shouting match. Many teams write that into an error budget policy: above 100% consumed, feature work pauses until the budget recovers or the causes are fixed.

Severity levels: sorting by impact, not by noise

A severity level is a label that says how bad an incident is and therefore how loudly to respond. The key rule: severity is decided by customer impact, never by how scary the error message looks or how senior the person shouting is. A typical ladder has four rungs. SEV1: a core function is down for many users, data is being lost, or customer data is exposed; page people immediately and give updates every 30 minutes. SEV2: a core function is badly degraded or one important segment is down; page the on-call and update hourly. SEV3: a minor feature is broken or a workaround exists; handle in working hours. SEV4: cosmetic or internal-only; put it in the backlog. You can raise or lower severity as facts arrive, and you should. Starting high and downgrading is cheap. Starting low and discovering an hour later that it was a SEV1 is not.

A first severity classifier
def severity(pct_users_hit, core_path, data_loss, workaround): if data_loss or (core_path and pct_users_hit >= 25): return "SEV1" if core_path and pct_users_hit >= 5: return "SEV2" if pct_users_hit >= 5 and not workaround: return "SEV2" if pct_users_hit > 0 and not workaround: return "SEV3" return "SEV4" print("checkout fails for 12% of users :", severity(12, True, False, False)) print("orders silently dropped :", severity(1, True, True, False)) print("export button fails for 2% :", severity(2, False, False, False)) print("About-page typo :", severity(0, False, False, True))
checkout fails for 12% of users : SEV2 orders silently dropped : SEV1 export button fails for 2% : SEV3 About-page typo : SEV4
Severity ladder and what each rung triggers
SEV1  core path down for many / data loss / breach

SEV2  core path degraded / big segment down

⚠️
Common mistakes with SLOs. (1) Measuring the server instead of the user: a service that answers 200 with an empty basket is "up" on a dashboard and broken for the customer. (2) Choosing 100%: it makes every deploy a violation. (3) One SLO for everything: checkout deserves 99.9%, a nightly report might be fine at 99%. (4) Setting severity by feeling. Write the rules down, like the classifier above, and argue about the rules in daylight, not at 3 a.m.
Check yourself: A month has 43.2 minutes of budget at 99.9%. A 30-minute full outage happens on day 3. What fraction is spent, and what does that mean for the rest of the month?
30 / 43.2 is about 69% spent in the first 10% of the window. The burn rate for the month is roughly 7x, so the team is in a "slow down and fix reliability first" position.
Check yourself: Why should the SLA be looser than the SLO?
The SLO is your early-warning line. If you breach it you notice and fix things while customers still have not been owed anything. If both were equal you would only find out at the moment you start paying refunds.

Recap
  • An SLI measures user experience as good events over valid events; an SLO is the target; an SLA is a contract with penalties.
  • The error budget is 1 minus the SLO. It makes reliability a number you can spend.
  • Burn rate (observed error rate divided by allowed rate) tells you how urgent a problem is.
  • Severity comes from customer impact and rules written in advance; adjust it as facts arrive.
4.2 — On-Call Basics: Being the Person the Pager Finds

A system with users needs a human who will look at it when it breaks, even at night. On-call is the duty of being reachable and ready to respond during a set period. A pager is whatever wakes you: today a phone app that makes a loud noise, historically a beeper. A rotation is the schedule that shares the duty, for example one person per week, with a second person as a secondary who backs up the first if they miss a page. Done well, on-call is a calm, boring, well-supported job. Done badly, it burns people out, and burned-out people make the worst decisions during the incidents that matter.

The quality of on-call is almost entirely decided by the quality of the alerts. Imagine Ben inherits a rotation where the pager fires 40 times a week and 33 of them need no action: a disk that is 81% full again, a health check that flaps for one minute. After two weeks Ben stops looking closely. That is alert fatigue, and it is dangerous because the one real page is buried among the false ones. The rule to remember: a page must be urgent, actionable and about user impact. If nothing needs doing right now, it should be a ticket or a dashboard, not a page. If a human cannot do anything useful, the alert should be deleted or automated. The next block measures this from an alert log.

How noisy is this pager? Score each alert by actionability
alerts = [ # (name, times_paged, times_action_needed) ("checkout_error_rate_high", 6, 5), ("disk_usage_over_80", 14, 0), ("api_latency_p99_slow_burn", 5, 4), ("cpu_spike_1min", 11, 1), ("queue_depth_growing", 4, 3), ] total_pages = sum(p for _, p, _ in alerts) total_real = sum(a for _, _, a in alerts) print(f"pages: {total_pages}, needed action: {total_real} ({total_real/total_pages:.0%} actionable)") for name, p, a in sorted(alerts, key=lambda x: x[2] / x[1]): verdict = "DELETE or demote to ticket" if a / p < 0.25 else "keep" print(f" {name:<28} {a}/{p} actionable -> {verdict}")
pages: 40, needed action: 13 (32% actionable) disk_usage_over_80 0/14 actionable -> DELETE or demote to ticket cpu_spike_1min 1/11 actionable -> DELETE or demote to ticket queue_depth_growing 3/4 actionable -> keep api_latency_p99_slow_burn 4/5 actionable -> keep checkout_error_rate_high 5/6 actionable -> keep

What Python does, line by line. Each tuple records how often an alert paged and how often a human really had to act. The two sum(...) lines add up the columns; the generator unpacks each tuple and ignores the fields marked _. Sorting by a / p puts the worst offenders first. The output says only 13 of 40 pages needed action (32%), and two alerts, the disk warning and the one-minute CPU spike, are responsible for 25 of the 40 pages. Fixing just those two removes more than half the noise. Teams call this a pager review, and the good ones do it weekly.

What good on-call looks like

A healthy rotation has five habits. First, a small, fair schedule: no one is on call more than about one week in four, and there is a clear swap process for holidays and illness. Second, a written handoff at the end of each shift: what fired, what is still open, what to watch. Third, an escalation path so nobody is alone: if the primary does not acknowledge in five minutes, the secondary is paged, then the team lead. Fourth, permission to act: the person on call may roll back, flip a feature flag or page another team without asking for approval first. Fifth, time back: a page at 2 a.m. earns a later start the next morning. None of this is generosity. Tired engineers cause the next incident.

When the page arrives, the first minute has a script. Acknowledge it so others know a human has it. Look at the alert's linked dashboard and runbook (more on those in 4.7). Ask "is this affecting customers right now?" and pick a provisional severity using the rules from 4.1. If you are not sure, treat it as one level worse and adjust later. If it is bigger than you can handle in ten minutes, declare an incident and pull in help. Asking for help early is a senior habit; waiting to be sure before asking is a junior one.

⚠️
Common mistakes. (1) Alerting on causes (CPU high, disk 80%) instead of symptoms (checkout errors, slow pages): causes often do not hurt anyone. (2) Paging on something with no runbook, so the person on call must invent the response at 3 a.m. (3) Fixing an alert by raising its threshold until it never fires, which hides the problem. (4) Treating "I was too embarrassed to wake someone" as a virtue. It is how a SEV2 becomes a SEV1.
Check yourself: An alert paged 12 times last month and nobody did anything any time. What are your two options?
Delete it, or demote it to a ticket or dashboard. A page that never needs action is noise, and noise trains people to ignore the pager.
Check yourself: Why alert on "checkout error rate above 2%" rather than "CPU above 90%"?
The error rate measures what customers feel (a symptom). High CPU is a possible cause that may hurt nobody, and a real problem can exist with normal CPU.

Try it. Add an alert to the list that paged 8 times and needed action 2 times. Where does it land in the sorted list, and would you keep it? Then change the threshold from 0.25 to 0.5 and see which verdicts change.

Recap
  • On-call is a shared, scheduled duty with a primary, a secondary and an escalation path.
  • Pages must be urgent, actionable and about user impact. Everything else is a ticket or a dashboard.
  • Review the pager weekly: delete or demote alerts that do not lead to action.
  • On call you may act (roll back, flag off, page others) without asking permission, and asking for help early is a strength.
4.3 — The Incident Lifecycle: Detect, Triage, Mitigate, Communicate, Resolve, Learn

An incident is any event that harms, or threatens to harm, users or the business and needs a coordinated response. Every incident, whether it is a bad deploy or a cloud region failing, moves through the same six stages. Knowing them by heart means that at 3 a.m., when your brain is slow, the next step is already decided.

The six stages
 1 DETECT ---> 2 TRIAGE ---> 3 MITIGATE ---> 4 COMMUNICATE (runs alongside 2-5)
   alert or      how bad?      stop the harm,      status updates on a fixed rhythm
   report        who leads?    do not wait to
                               understand                |
 5 RESOLVE <-----------------------------------------------+
   fix the cause,
   confirm recovery      ---> 6 LEARN  (blameless postmortem, action items)
Mitigate first, understand later

The most important idea in incident response is that mitigation (stopping the harm) is a different job from resolution (fixing the cause), and mitigation comes first. Picture a bathroom flooding. You do not start by diagnosing why the pipe burst. You turn off the water at the main valve, and then you investigate. In software the main valves are: roll back the last deploy, flip a feature flag off, fail over to a healthy region, shed load by rejecting low-priority traffic, or scale up for a few hours. These are blunt, temporary, and fine. The beginner's instinct is the opposite: "let me find the real bug first". That instinct keeps customers broken for an extra hour while an engineer reads code.

The four numbers that summarise how well a team did are all measured from timestamps. Time to detect is from when the harm began until someone knew. Time to acknowledge is from the page until a human had it. Time to mitigate is from the start of harm until customers stopped feeling it. Time to resolve is until the underlying cause was fixed. Averages of these over many incidents are the famous MTTD, MTTA, MTTM and MTTR. Watch for one trap: "R" can mean recover, repair, respond or resolve, so always say which one you mean. Let us compute them from a real-looking timeline.

Lifecycle timing from a timeline
from datetime import datetime FMT = "%H:%M" events = [("14:02", "harm_start"), ("14:11", "alert_fired"), ("14:13", "acked"), ("14:19", "incident_declared"), ("14:26", "mitigated"), ("15:40", "resolved")] t = {name: datetime.strptime(ts, FMT) for ts, name in events} minutes = lambda a, b: int((t[b] - t[a]).total_seconds() // 60) print("time to detect :", minutes("harm_start", "alert_fired"), "min") print("time to acknowledge:", minutes("alert_fired", "acked"), "min") print("time to mitigate :", minutes("harm_start", "mitigated"), "min (customers hurt this long)") print("time to resolve :", minutes("harm_start", "resolved"), "min") print("gap after mitigation:", minutes("mitigated", "resolved"), "min of calm cleanup")
time to detect : 9 min time to acknowledge: 2 min time to mitigate : 24 min (customers hurt this long) time to resolve : 98 min gap after mitigation: 74 min of calm cleanup

Read it like a story. Nine minutes passed before any alert fired; that is the detection gap and a candidate for an alert improvement. Customers were hurt for 24 minutes in total. The fix took another 74 minutes, but nobody was suffering during that time, so the team could work carefully instead of frantically. That difference, hurt customers versus an open ticket, is exactly why the mitigate-first rule exists.

Triage, communicate, resolve, learn

Triage means quickly answering four questions: what is broken, who is affected, how bad is it (severity), and who is in charge. Write down your best guess even if it is uncertain; you will revise it. Communicate is not a stage that happens after the work; it runs in parallel, on a fixed rhythm (every 30 minutes for a SEV1), even if the update says "no change, still investigating". Silence makes stakeholders invent their own theories and interrupt the people fixing the problem. Resolve means the cause is fixed and you have verified recovery from the customer's point of view: error rate back to baseline, a test purchase succeeds, the queue has drained. Learn means a blameless postmortem within about a week, while memories are fresh, with action items that someone owns. An incident with no learning step is just a repeat waiting to happen.

⚠️
Common mistakes. (1) Declaring victory when the graph looks better, before confirming from the user's side. (2) Rolling back and then forgetting to keep the evidence: save logs, graphs and the bad build before they rotate away. (3) Making several changes at once during mitigation, so nobody knows which one worked. (4) Skipping the postmortem because "it was just a small one". Small incidents are the cheapest place to learn.
Check yourself: Deploy at 10:00 broke checkout. At 10:12 you can either roll back (5 minutes) or debug the failing query (unknown time). Which first, and why?
Roll back first. Mitigation stops customer harm quickly and preserves the ability to debug calmly. You can inspect the bad version in staging after customers are safe.
Check yourself: A graph shows errors dropping after your rollback. Is the incident resolved?
Not yet. It is mitigated. Resolve when the cause is fixed and recovery is verified from the user's side, for example a real test checkout works and the error rate stays at baseline.

Try it. Move mitigated to 14:50 and rerun. Which number changed, and which did not? Then add a customer_report event at 14:05 and compute how much sooner customers detected the problem than your alerts did.

Recap
  • Six stages: detect, triage, mitigate, communicate (in parallel), resolve, learn.
  • Mitigate first with blunt tools (rollback, flag off, failover, shed load); understand later.
  • Measure time to detect, acknowledge, mitigate and resolve from timestamps, and say which "R" you mean.
  • Communicate on a fixed rhythm even when nothing changed, and verify recovery from the user's side.
4.4 — Incident Roles: Commander, Communications, Operations

Picture a large incident with eight engineers in one chat. Everyone is investigating, three people propose different fixes, two of them run commands on production at the same time, and the CEO's assistant asks for an update every four minutes. Nothing is being coordinated. The cure is borrowed from emergency services: give people roles, so that each job has one owner and the people fixing the problem are protected from interruption.

The incident commander (IC) runs the incident. The IC does not type fixes. Their job is to keep the big picture: what do we know, what are we trying, who is doing what, and what is the severity. They make decisions when the group is split ("we roll back, now"), and they hand the role to someone else if the incident runs longer than a few hours. The operations lead (sometimes called the tech lead) directs the hands-on investigation and changes, and is the only person who changes production, so two people never collide. The communications lead writes the status updates to customers and internal stakeholders and is the single voice outward. A scribe records the timeline in real time; that record is the raw material of the postmortem. In a small incident one person may wear several hats, but the hats should still be named aloud: "I am IC and scribe, Chen is ops."

Who talks to whom during a SEV1
             +------------------+
   pages --> | Incident         | <-- severity, priorities, hand-offs
             | Commander (IC)   |
             +---+----------+---+
                 |          |
        tasks    |          |   facts to publish
                 v          v
        +--------------+  +-------------------+       customers,
        | Ops lead     |  | Comms lead        | ----> support,
        | + responders |  | (status updates)  |       leadership
        +------+-------+  +-------------------+
               | events
               v
        +--------------+
        | Scribe       |  ->  timeline for the postmortem
        +--------------+

Why does this work? Because it separates deciding, doing, telling and recording, four activities that interfere with each other when one person does them all. A responder who is deep in a database prompt cannot also draft a customer update, and an update written in a hurry is often wrong. Roles also fix the "bystander effect": when everyone is looking at the problem, everyone assumes someone else is telling the customers. Naming the comms lead makes it certain.

Running the channel

Practical rules: open one dedicated channel per incident and pin the summary, severity and current roles at the top. Say what you are about to do before you do it ("Chen: rolling back checkout to build 412 now"), and what happened after ("rollback done, error rate falling"). Side conversations move to a thread. The IC asks for a status every 15 minutes on a big incident: "what do we know, what are we trying, what do we need?" If someone new joins, the IC or scribe gives them a one-line summary instead of making them read 200 messages. The block below checks a channel log against two of these rules: every change announced by exactly one person in the ops role, and status updates on schedule.

Check a channel log against the roles
ROLES = {"asha": "IC", "chen": "ops", "dana": "comms", "eli": "scribe", "farah": "responder"} log = [(0, "asha", "declare SEV2 checkout errors"), (3, "chen", "CHANGE rollback to 412"), (5, "farah", "CHANGE restart pool"), (9, "dana", "UPDATE investigating"), (41, "dana", "UPDATE mitigated"), (43, "eli", "note: timeline started")] problems = [] for minute, who, msg in log: if msg.startswith("CHANGE") and ROLES[who] != "ops": problems.append(f"minute {minute}: {who} ({ROLES[who]}) changed production, only ops should") updates = [m for m, w, msg in log if msg.startswith("UPDATE")] for a, b in zip(updates, updates[1:]): if b - a > 30: problems.append(f"minutes {a}-{b}: {b - a} min between updates, SEV2 cadence is 30") print("\n".join(problems) or "roles respected")

⚠️
Common mistakes. (1) The most senior engineer becomes IC and then also debugs, so nobody coordinates. Keep the IC's hands off the keyboard. (2) No hand-off: after four hours the IC is tired and decisions degrade; rotate and say "you are now IC" explicitly. (3) Several people talk to customers with different words. (4) Treating roles as rank. The IC is a job for the duration of the incident, not a promotion.
Check yourself: Why should the IC not run the fix themselves?
Because holding the big picture, prioritising and deciding are full-time jobs during an incident. If the IC dives into the fix, nobody is watching severity, communication, or whether the team is going in circles.
Check yourself: Two engineers both restart services at the same time and the graph gets worse. Which role rule was broken?
Only the operations lead changes production. Two uncoordinated changers make it impossible to know which action caused which effect.

Recap
  • Give each job one owner: incident commander, operations lead, communications lead and scribe.
  • The IC decides and coordinates; the IC does not debug. Only ops changes production.
  • Announce changes before and after doing them; keep one channel per incident.
  • Hand over the IC role after a few hours, out loud.
4.5 — A Debugging Method: Hypotheses, "What Changed?", USE and RED, Bisecting

Here is how debugging goes without a method. Checkout errors start. Someone says "it is probably the database". Someone else restarts a service "just in case". A third person reads a 2,000-line file. Forty minutes later the errors are still there, three things were changed, and nobody can say which of them mattered. Guessing is not the problem; hunches are useful. The problem is guessing without writing anything down and changing several things at once.

The method fits in one sentence: form a hypothesis, predict what you should see if it is true, check cheaply, and record the result. A hypothesis is a specific, testable claim such as "the errors began when the connection pool was shrunk". The prediction is what would have to be true: pool metrics show waiting requests starting at 13:40. The cheap check reads a graph or a log line rather than changing production. Ruling a hypothesis out is progress, just as good as ruling one in, because the search space shrinks. Doctors call this a differential diagnosis, and you can keep the same habit: list several candidates, order them by likelihood and cheapness to test, and cross them off.

The hypothesis loop
   observe symptom -> list hypotheses -> pick the cheapest to test
          ^                                    |
          |                                    v
   record result  <-  run ONE check  <-  predict what you would see
   (ruled out = progress)      |
                               +--> confirmed? -> mitigate / fix -> verify from the user side
The first question: what changed?

Systems that ran fine yesterday and fail today usually broke because something changed: a deploy, a configuration edit, a feature flag, a dependency upgrade, a certificate expiring, a traffic spike, a data shape you had never seen. So the highest-yield first step is to build a list of everything that changed near the start of the symptoms and rank it. Note the phrase "near the start": you need the moment the trouble began, read from a graph, not the moment somebody noticed it.

Rank recent changes by how close they are to the symptom start
def mins(hhmm): h, m = hhmm.split(":"); return int(h) * 60 + int(m) changes = [("13:05", "deploy", "catalog v88", "catalog"), ("13:40", "config", "checkout.pool_size 40 -> 10", "checkout"), ("13:52", "flag", "new_banner on", "web"), ("14:01", "deploy", "search v12", "search"), ("14:10", "infra", "node pool patched", "all")] symptom_start, symptom_system = mins("13:43"), "checkout" suspects = [] for ts, kind, what, system in changes: lag = symptom_start - mins(ts) if lag < 0: continue # a change AFTER the symptom cannot be its cause scoped = system in (symptom_system, "all") suspects.append((not scoped, lag, ts, kind, what, scoped)) # on-path first, then closest for _, lag, ts, kind, what, scoped in sorted(suspects): print(f"{ts} {kind:<6} {what:<28} {lag:>3} min before symptom, touches checkout path: {scoped}")

Two rules are built in. A change that happened after the symptom began cannot be the cause, so it is dropped (the 13:52 flag and the later deploys are ruled out). And a change to a system on the failing path is a stronger suspect than one elsewhere. The top suspect, the pool-size change three minutes before the errors began, becomes the first hypothesis to test. It is not proven. Correlation in time is a lead, not a verdict.

Two checklists so you never stare at a blank dashboard

When "what changed?" gives nothing, use a systematic view of the system. For services (things that answer requests) use the RED method: Rate (requests per second), Errors (how many fail), Duration (how long they take, as percentiles). For resources (CPU, memory, disk, connection pools, queues) use the USE method: Utilization (how busy, as a percentage), Saturation (work waiting because the resource is full), Errors (failed operations). RED tells you which service is unhealthy. USE tells you which resource under it is the reason. The idea is to ask the same three questions of everything instead of trusting your favourite suspect.

RED for services, USE for resources
def pct(sorted_vals, p): # nearest-rank percentile return sorted_vals[max(0, -(-len(sorted_vals) * p // 100) - 1)] # 100 requests to two routes; deterministic latencies, checkout is slow and failing reqs = [("/search", 200, 30 + i % 20) for i in range(60)] reqs += [("/checkout", 500 if i % 4 == 0 else 200, 900 + 40 * (i % 10)) for i in range(40)] for route in ("/search", "/checkout"): rows = [r for r in reqs if r[0] == route] lat = sorted(r[2] for r in rows) err = sum(r[1] >= 500 for r in rows) / len(rows) print(f"RED {route:<10} rate={len(rows)} errors={err:.0%} p50={pct(lat,50)}ms p95={pct(lat,95)}ms") resources = {"cpu": (0.42, 0), "memory": (0.61, 0), "db_pool": (1.00, 37)} # utilization, waiting for name, (util, waiting) in resources.items(): flag = "SATURATED" if waiting else "ok" print(f"USE {name:<8} utilization={util:.0%} waiting={waiting} -> {flag}")

RED points at /checkout (25% errors, p95 far above search). USE then points below it: CPU and memory have room, but the database connection pool is 100% used with 37 requests waiting, a textbook saturation signature. Notice how this agrees with the top suspect from "what changed": pool size was cut from 40 to 10. Two independent methods, one answer. That is how you build confidence before you touch anything.

Bisecting: when the change is somewhere in a big range

Sometimes you know "it worked at build 1 and fails at build 64" but not which change in between. Do not read all 63. Bisect: test the middle, discard the half that cannot contain the bug, repeat. Every step halves the range, so 64 candidates need at most 6 tests. git bisect automates this for commits, and the idea also works for config values, input rows, and feature flags: turn half of them off and see if the problem remains.

Binary search for the first bad build
def is_bad(build): # in real life: deploy the build and run a check return build >= 37 # the hidden truth: build 37 introduced the bug tests = 0 lo_good, hi_bad = 1, 64 # invariant: lo_good is good, hi_bad is bad while hi_bad - lo_good > 1: mid = (lo_good + hi_bad) // 2 tests += 1 if is_bad(mid): hi_bad = mid else: lo_good = mid print(f"test build {mid:>2}: {'bad ' if is_bad(mid) else 'good'} -> range now {lo_good}..{hi_bad}") print(f"first bad build: {hi_bad}, found in {tests} tests (a linear scan needs up to 63)")

⚠️
Common mistakes. (1) Changing two things per experiment: if the problem disappears you do not know which one fixed it. (2) Confirmation bias: looking only for evidence that supports your favourite theory; write down what would disprove it. (3) Testing the fix in a way that cannot fail (restarting and seeing "it works now" often means the symptom hid, not that the cause was fixed). (4) Bisecting with a flaky test: a test that is sometimes wrong sends you into the wrong half. Repeat the test until you trust it.
Check yourself: The errors began at 13:43. A deploy finished at 13:55. Can it be the cause?
No. A change that happens after the symptom began cannot have caused the start of the symptom. It might be making things worse, or it might be unrelated, but the trigger is earlier.

About 10, because 2^10 = 1,024. Each test halves the range.

Recap
  • Debug with hypotheses: predict what you would see, check cheaply, write down the result.
  • First ask "what changed?", ranked by timing and by whether it touches the failing path.
  • RED (rate, errors, duration) for services; USE (utilization, saturation, errors) for resources.
  • Bisect large ranges: each test halves the search. Change one thing at a time.
4.6 — Reading Logs, Metrics and Traces

The three sources of evidence in a running system are called the three pillars of observability, and each answers a different question. Metrics are numbers over time (requests per second, p95 latency); they answer "is something wrong, and when did it start?" and are cheap to store. Logs are records of individual events (a line saying an order failed and why); they answer "what exactly happened?" and are detailed but bulky. Traces follow one request as it hops between services, with the time spent in each hop; they answer "where did the time go?". A good investigation moves across them: metric to find the start, trace to find the slow hop, logs to find the reason.

Moving across the three pillars
  METRICS  "checkout p95 jumped at 13:42"        -> WHEN and HOW BIG
     |
     v
  TRACES   "2,010 of 2,400 ms in acquire_conn"   -> WHERE the time goes
     |
     v
  LOGS     "db timeout acquiring connection"     -> WHY, in words
Logs: find the first thing that went wrong

A pile of 10,000 error lines is usually the same three errors repeated, and most are symptoms. The line you want is the earliest error from the lowest layer. Downstream services shout because an upstream one failed. So parse, group by signature (the message with the variable parts like ids removed), and read the first occurrence of each. Here is a tiny fixture in the format many services use: timestamp, level, component, message.

Group log lines by error signature and find who complained first
import re from collections import Counter logs = """13:40:02 INFO checkout order 8841 created 13:40:31 WARN db pool waiting=3 max=10 13:41:07 ERROR db timeout acquiring connection after 2000ms 13:41:09 ERROR checkout order 8850 failed: upstream db timeout 13:41:10 ERROR checkout order 8851 failed: upstream db timeout 13:41:12 ERROR web 502 from checkout for /pay 13:41:15 ERROR db timeout acquiring connection after 2000ms 13:41:20 ERROR web 502 from checkout for /pay""".splitlines() sig = lambda msg: re.sub(r"\d+", "N", msg) first, count = {}, Counter() for line in logs: ts, level, comp, msg = line.split(None, 3) if level == "ERROR": key = (comp, sig(msg)); count[key] += 1; first.setdefault(key, ts) for (comp, msg), n in sorted(count.items(), key=lambda kv: first[kv[0]]): print(f"{first[(comp, msg)]} x{n} {comp}: {msg}")
13:41:07 x2 db: timeout acquiring connection after Nms 13:41:09 x2 checkout: order N failed: upstream db timeout 13:41:12 x2 web: N from checkout for /pay

Sorted by first appearance, the story reads itself: the database complained first (a WARN even earlier, at 13:40:31, said the pool was filling), then checkout, then the web tier. The web 502s that users see are the last link in a chain that started at the pool. If you had started from the most frequent error you might have investigated the wrong layer. The regex \d+ to N is the trick that makes "order 8850 failed" and "order 8851 failed" count as one signature.

Metrics: find the moment the line bent

Averages hide problems. If 95 requests take 50 ms and 5 take 5,000 ms, the average is 297 ms, which describes nobody's real experience. Use percentiles: p50 is the typical request, p95 the slow tail, p99 the very slow tail. Look at them per minute and find the step where the level changes. That minute is your incident's start, which you then compare against the change list from 4.5.

Find the minute a latency metric stepped up
# p95 latency per minute, 13:30 .. 13:49 (ms) p95 = [210, 205, 220, 215, 208, 212, 219, 214, 207, 216, 218, 220, 640, 1180, 1210, 1195, 1230, 1205, 1190, 1215] baseline = sum(p95[:8]) / 8 for i, v in enumerate(p95): if v > 2 * baseline: print(f"first minute above 2x baseline: 13:{30 + i} ({v} ms vs baseline {baseline:.0f} ms)") break after = sum(p95[13:]) / len(p95[13:]) print(f"level after the step: {after:.0f} ms, about {after / baseline:.1f}x baseline")
first minute above 2x baseline: 13:42 (640 ms vs baseline 213 ms) level after the step: 1204 ms, about 5.7x baseline

The step is at 13:42 (a partial-minute ramp), and the new plateau is about 5.7 times baseline. Two more cautions about metrics. Compare against the same time last week, not just against ten minutes ago, since traffic has daily and weekly rhythms. And check the resolution: a five-minute average can hide a two-minute outage entirely.

Traces: where did the time go?

A trace is a tree of spans; each span is one operation with a start and a duration, and children are calls made by the parent. The slow part of a request is the child with the largest self time: its own duration minus the time spent in its children. Big self time on a leaf usually means "this operation is the slow one"; a parent with big self time and no slow children means waiting inside itself (locks, queues, or garbage collection).

Find the span with the most self time
spans = [ # (id, parent, name, duration_ms) (1, None, "POST /checkout", 2400), (2, 1, "auth", 30), (3, 1, "cart.load", 90), (4, 1, "payment.charge", 2150), (5, 4, "db.acquire_connection", 2010), (6, 4, "db.insert_order", 60), ] child_time = {} for sid, parent, name, dur in spans: if parent: child_time[parent] = child_time.get(parent, 0) + dur self_time = {name: dur - child_time.get(sid, 0) for sid, parent, name, dur in spans} for name, t in sorted(self_time.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:3]: print(f"{name:<24} self time {t} ms")
db.acquire_connection self time 2010 ms POST /checkout self time 130 ms cart.load self time 90 ms

The output shows db.acquire_connection holding 2,010 of the 2,400 ms: the request spent nearly all its life waiting for a connection, not doing database work. That is the third confirmation of the same story (config change, saturated pool, log timeout) from three different sources. When independent pillars agree, you can act. When they disagree, that disagreement is the next thing to investigate.

⚠️
Common mistakes. (1) Mixing time zones: one dashboard in UTC and another in local time creates a phantom "cause" an hour earlier. Write UTC in every incident note. (2) Trusting a sampled trace as the whole picture when only 1% of requests are traced. (3) Reading the last error in a log instead of the first. (4) Treating "no errors in the logs" as "no problem": the failure might be a missing log line, such as a job that never ran.
Check yourself: Why is the most frequent error line often not the root cause?
It is frequently a downstream symptom repeated once per failed request. The root cause is typically an earlier, less frequent error from a lower layer.
Check yourself: p50 is 60 ms and p99 is 4,000 ms. Is the service healthy?
Not for the slowest 1% of users. Most requests are fine, but the tail is very slow, and the tail often includes your most important, heavy requests. Look at percentiles, not averages.

Recap
  • Metrics say when it started, traces say where the time went, logs say why.
  • In logs, group by signature and read the earliest error from the lowest layer.
  • Use percentiles per minute, look for the step, and compare with the same time last week.
  • In traces, rank spans by self time; write every timestamp in UTC.
4.7 — Runbooks and Automation

It is 3 a.m. and the alert says checkout_pool_saturated. Dana is on call for the first time. She does not know this system well. Should she have to read source code, search old chat threads, and ask a colleague who is asleep? A runbook is a short document, linked from the alert itself, that says what this alert means, how to confirm it, what to do first, how to check it worked, and when to escalate. It turns the knowledge of your best engineer into something a tired new person can follow. The test of a good runbook is that someone who did not write it can follow it half asleep.

Good runbooks are short and structured. They have: a title and the alert name so you can find them; what it means and who it hurts in two sentences; how to confirm with exact links or commands; first-response steps in order, safest first; how to verify the fix from the user's side; an escalation contact; and an owner and last-reviewed date. That last field matters: a runbook nobody has read for a year describes a system that no longer exists, and following stale steps can make things worse. Because runbooks are structured, they can be checked by a program, exactly like code.

A runbook linter: every alert needs a complete, fresh runbook
REQUIRED = ["meaning", "confirm", "steps", "verify", "escalate", "owner"] MAX_AGE_DAYS = 180 runbooks = { "checkout_pool_saturated": {"meaning": "DB pool full", "confirm": "dash/db-pool", "steps": ["raise pool_size", "rollback config"], "verify": "test checkout succeeds", "escalate": "db-team", "owner": "payments", "age_days": 40}, "queue_depth_growing": {"meaning": "workers behind", "steps": ["scale workers to 8"], "owner": "platform", "age_days": 400}, "disk_usage_over_80": {}, } for alert in ["checkout_pool_saturated", "queue_depth_growing", "disk_usage_over_80", "cert_expiring"]: rb = runbooks.get(alert) if rb is None: print(f"{alert}: NO RUNBOOK"); continue issues = [f"missing {k}" for k in REQUIRED if not rb.get(k)] if rb.get("age_days", 9999) > MAX_AGE_DAYS: issues.append(f"stale ({rb.get('age_days', '?')} days since review)") print(f"{alert}: {'ok' if not issues else '; '.join(issues)}")
checkout_pool_saturated: ok queue_depth_growing: missing confirm; missing verify; missing escalate; stale (400 days since review) disk_usage_over_80: missing meaning; missing confirm; missing steps; missing verify; missing escalate; missing owner; stale (? days since review) cert_expiring: NO RUNBOOK

The linter finds four different kinds of trouble: an alert with no runbook at all (cert_expiring), one with an empty stub, one that is incomplete and 400 days old, and one that passes. Run something like this in CI and "every page has a runbook" stops being a wish. Notice that the design keeps the data (a dict) separate from the checking. That is what makes runbooks lint-able and later automatable.

From document to automation, one careful rung at a time

If a runbook step is the same every time ("restart the worker", "clear the stuck queue"), a computer should do it. But automation in incident response has a nasty failure mode: the script that "helps" makes the incident worse at machine speed. So climb a ladder. Rung one: a written step. Rung two: a script a human runs on purpose. Rung three: a script the system suggests and a human approves with one click. Rung four: full automation, but only for actions that are safe, reversible, and limited by guardrails. Each rung is earned by the previous one working reliably many times.

The automation ladder
 4  auto-remediate     safe + reversible + rate-limited, pages a human if it fires often
 3  suggest + approve  bot proposes "restart worker 3?", human clicks yes
 2  script             `./restart_worker.sh 3` run by a person, with dry-run
 1  written step       runbook line: "restart worker 3, then check queue depth"
        ^ climb only when the rung below has worked many times

The important guardrails are these. A rate limit ("at most 3 restarts per hour") so a crash loop pages a human instead of restarting forever. A blast radius cap ("never restart more than 20% of the fleet at once"). A dry run mode that prints what would happen. And an audit log, because during the postmortem you will ask "what did the robot do at 03:12?". The block simulates an auto-restarter with the first guardrail.

Auto-restart with a rate limit, escalating to a human
class AutoRestarter: def __init__(self, max_per_hour=3): self.max_per_hour, self.history, self.log = max_per_hour, [], [] def on_unhealthy(self, minute): recent = [m for m in self.history if minute - m < 60] if len(recent) >= self.max_per_hour: self.log.append(f"minute {minute}: limit reached -> PAGE A HUMAN (crash loop?)") return self.history.append(minute) self.log.append(f"minute {minute}: restarted worker ({len(recent) + 1}/{self.max_per_hour} this hour)") bot = AutoRestarter() for minute in (5, 12, 20, 27, 90): bot.on_unhealthy(minute) print("\n".join(bot.log))
minute 5: restarted worker (1/3 this hour) minute 12: restarted worker (2/3 this hour) minute 20: restarted worker (3/3 this hour) minute 27: limit reached -> PAGE A HUMAN (crash loop?) minute 90: restarted worker (1/3 this hour)

The first three failures are absorbed silently. The fourth, at minute 27, would be a fourth restart within an hour, so the bot stops and wakes a person: repeated crashes mean the restart is hiding a real bug. At minute 90 the old restarts have aged out of the window, so it restarts again. Silent forever-restarts are exactly how a slow memory leak survives for months.

⚠️
Common mistakes. (1) Runbooks that say "investigate" instead of naming the first command. (2) Automation with no off switch: always provide a way to disable it fast. (3) Steps that are safe only in the order given but are written as a loose list; number them. (4) Never rehearsing: run a runbook in a game day (a practice incident) and fix every step that surprises the reader.
Check yourself: What is the minimum a runbook should tell a stranger about an alert?
What it means and who it hurts, how to confirm it, the first safe steps in order, how to verify the fix from the user's side, who to escalate to, plus an owner and review date.
Check yourself: Why must automated remediation have a rate limit?
Otherwise a crash loop is restarted forever and hidden. The limit forces a human to look when the same fault repeats.

Try it. Change max_per_hour to 2 and predict which minute triggers the page. Then add a "verify" field to the queue_depth_growing runbook and watch its issue list shrink.

Recap
  • A runbook is a short, structured, linked-from-the-alert guide anyone can follow half asleep.
  • Because it is structured, you can lint it in CI: every alert has a complete, recently reviewed runbook.
  • Automate in rungs: written step, script, suggest-and-approve, guarded auto-remediation.
  • Guardrails: rate limits, blast-radius caps, dry runs, audit logs and an off switch.
4.8 — Communication Templates

During an incident the engineers fixing the problem are busy and everyone else is anxious. Support wants to know what to tell customers. Leadership wants to know whether to worry. Customers are refreshing the page. If you do not write the story, others will write it for you, usually a scarier one. Good communication during an incident is not decoration: it protects the people fixing the problem from interruption, and it protects trust, which lasts longer than any outage.

The remedy is to use templates decided in advance, so that at 3 a.m. you fill in blanks rather than compose prose. Every status update answers the same five questions in the same order: what is happening (in customer words), who is affected, what we are doing, what we do not know yet, and when the next update will come. Two habits make updates trustworthy. First, promise a next-update time and keep it, even if the update is "no change". Second, state facts and label guesses: "we believe" for hypotheses, "confirmed" only for verified things. Do not say "root cause" in the middle of an incident; you rarely know it yet.

Template: the five-part status update
[Investigating | Identified | Monitoring | Resolved] {short title} What is happening: {customer-visible effect, in plain words} Who is affected: {share of users, regions, features} What we are doing: {current action, e.g. "rolling back a configuration change"} What we do not know yet: {open questions} Next update: {clock time, in UTC} or sooner if things change

The word in square brackets is the status, and it matters. Investigating means you know there is a problem but not the cause. Identified means you know what is wrong and are fixing it. Monitoring means a fix is in and you are watching. Resolved means it is over and verified. Customers use the status to decide whether to wait or work around it, so change it only when the meaning changes. Here is a checker that catches common template mistakes, the sort of thing an incident bot can do before an update is posted.

Lint a status update before posting it
import re def lint(update): problems = [] if not re.search(r"^\[(Investigating|Identified|Monitoring|Resolved)\]", update): problems.append("missing status word") if not re.search(r"Next update: \d\d:\d\d UTC", update): problems.append("no next-update time in UTC") if re.search(r"root cause|caused by|human error|forgot|mistake", update, re.I): problems.append("blame or unverified cause in a live update") if "Who is affected" not in update: problems.append("does not say who is affected") return problems good = "[Identified] Checkout errors\nWho is affected: about 1 in 4 checkouts\nWhat we are doing: rolling back a change\nNext update: 14:30 UTC" bad = "Checkout is down because Ben made a mistake with the config. Will update later." for name, u in (("good", good), ("bad", bad)): print(name, "->", lint(u) or "ok")
good -> ok bad -> ['missing status word', 'no next-update time in UTC', 'blame or unverified cause in a live update', 'does not say who is affected']
Different audiences, different messages

One incident, three audiences. Customers get the effect, the workaround if there is one, and an apology when the incident is over, in plain words with no internal names. Support and sales get the same facts plus a line to use ("we are aware, engineers are working on it, next update at 14:30") so nobody improvises. Leadership gets severity, business impact, ETA-with-uncertainty, and any decision needed from them. Notice that the facts are the same and the framing differs. The comms lead writes all three from one shared source of truth, so they never contradict each other.

Finally, the closing message. When resolved, say what was affected and for how long, thank customers for their patience, and promise a summary. If customers were materially affected (money, data), publish a short public postmortem within a few days. A sample of good final text is: "Between 13:43 and 14:07 UTC, about 1 in 4 checkout attempts failed. The problem was caused by a configuration change, which we reversed. Every affected customer can retry; no payments were taken twice. We are adding checks so this type of change cannot go out untested."

Compute the next-update time from severity

Recap
  • Prepare templates: what is happening, who is affected, what we are doing, what we do not know, when next.
  • Status words (Investigating, Identified, Monitoring, Resolved) have fixed meanings.
  • Label guesses, avoid blame and the phrase "root cause" mid-incident, and keep your update promises.

4.9 — Blameless Postmortems: Timeline, Impact, Causes, Actions

The incident is over. Now comes the step that decides whether it will happen again: the postmortem (also called an incident review or retrospective), a written analysis of what happened, why, and what will change. The aim is learning, not punishment. It is blameless, which does not mean nobody is responsible. It means we assume everyone acted sensibly with the information and tools they had at that moment, and we ask what about the system made the mistake easy to make and hard to notice. If people are punished for honest mistakes, they hide them, and hidden mistakes are the ones that recur. If Ben knows he will be thanked for describing exactly what he did and thought, the whole team learns from it.

A good postmortem has a fixed skeleton. Summary: three sentences a busy person can read. Impact: numbers, not adjectives. Timeline: timestamped facts. Detection and response: how long each stage took. Causes: trigger, latent condition and contributing factors. What went well, what went badly, where we got lucky. Action items: owned, dated, verifiable. The next parts explain the trickiest pieces.

Timelines: facts in one clock

A timeline lists what happened, in order, with the time in one time zone (UTC). It contains facts, not opinions, and includes the events before the incident (the change) and after (verification), plus the moments of human decision ("13:58 IC declared SEV2"). Timelines are built by merging several sources: deploy history, alert logs, chat, dashboards. Those sources disagree on time zones and clocks, which is why the first job is normalising them, as in this block.

Merge events from several sources into one UTC timeline

Root cause versus contributing factors

Beginners write one "root cause". Real incidents almost never have one. Think of the Swiss cheese model: a system has many layers of protection (review, tests, canary release, alerts, runbooks), each with holes. An incident happens when the holes line up. So describe the trigger (the event that set it off: a config edit), the latent condition (the flaw waiting there: pool of 10 is too small for peak traffic, and nothing said so), and contributing factors (why it was not caught or was slow to fix: config changes skip the canary, no alert on pool saturation, a runbook missing). Fixing only the trigger prevents that exact edit; fixing the latent condition and the contributing factors prevents the family of similar incidents.

The 5 whys, and how it goes wrong

The 5 whys is a habit of asking "why?" repeatedly to move from a symptom toward deeper causes. It is useful, but it has four well-known traps. It stops at a person: "why did checkout break? Because Ben changed the config" is where a blaming review ends; a blameless one asks why that change was possible and unguarded. It follows one line when reality is a web: at every step several answers may be true, so branch. It stops too early at a comfortable answer like "we need to be more careful", which is not actionable. And it invents certainty: each "because" must be backed by evidence from the timeline, not by plausible storytelling. A better rule than "ask five times" is "keep asking until the answer is something you can change in the system".

Action items that actually get done

A postmortem without action items is a story. A postmortem with vague ones ("improve monitoring", "be more careful") is also a story. 4.11 — Preventing Recurrence: Alerts, Tests and Guardrails

The action items of a postmortem fall into a few families, and each family has its own craft. This section turns the three most common ones into working code: alerts that tell you sooner, tests that remember the incident forever, and guardrails that make the dangerous action hard or impossible. Think of them as three nets at different heights: the alert catches what escapes, the test catches it before release, and the guardrail stops it from being possible at all. The strongest fixes are the ones that make the mistake impossible rather than merely detectable.

Alerts that fire on burn rate, not on single blips

Back in 4.1 you met the burn rate. An alert that fires when the burn rate is high is better than a fixed threshold, because it scales with your SLO. The standard recipe uses two windows. A fast-burn alert pages if the burn rate is above about 14 over both a 1-hour and a 5-minute window: at that pace 2% of the month's budget is gone in an hour. A slow-burn alert opens a ticket if the burn is above about 3 over 6 hours: a leak that would drain the budget in ten days. Requiring the short window too means the alert resets quickly after you fix the problem, instead of staying red for an hour.

Multi-window burn-rate alert on per-minute error rates
SLO = 0.999 allowed = 1 - SLO def burn(window): # average error rate over the window / allowed rate return (sum(window) / len(window)) / allowed # error rate per minute for the last 60 minutes: healthy, then 25% errors for 24 minutes, then fixed errors = [0.0004] * 20 + [0.25] * 24 + [0.0004] * 16 for minute in (30, 50, 60): w60, w5 = errors[max(0, minute - 60):minute], errors[minute - 5:minute] fast = burn(w60) > 14 and burn(w5) > 14 print(f"minute {minute}: burn(1h)={burn(w60):6.1f} burn(5m)={burn(w5):6.1f} -> {'PAGE' if fast else 'quiet'}")
minute 30: burn(1h)= 83.6 burn(5m)= 250.0 -> PAGE minute 50: burn(1h)= 120.2 burn(5m)= 0.4 -> quiet minute 60: burn(1h)= 100.2 burn(5m)= 0.4 -> quiet

At minute 30 the incident has been running for 10 minutes, and both windows are well above 14, so the page fires. At minute 50 the long window is still high but the 5-minute window has fully recovered, so it correctly goes quiet. This is the "reset quickly" benefit. Compare that to the nine-minute detection gap in the worked postmortem, where the alert waited for five minutes of >5% errors.

Regression tests: the incident becomes a test that never forgets

Every incident tells you exactly one thing your tests did not cover. Turn it into a regression test: a test that fails on the broken behaviour and passes on the fixed one. Write the failing test first, watch it fail on the old code, then make it pass. In our incident the missing rule was "a production pool must be big enough for the expected concurrency". The guard is a validation function plus tests for the exact bad value that caused the outage.

Guardrails: canaries, limits and defaults that are safe

A guardrail is a rule built into the system or pipeline that stops a dangerous action or shrinks its effect. Examples: a canary release sends a change to 5% of traffic first and compares it with the old version; feature flags with a kill switch; rate limits; a confirmation for production-destroying commands; read-only credentials by default; and defaults that fail safe. A canary is the guardrail that would have saved the worked incident, because 5% of traffic saturating a pool would have been visible in minutes at a tiny cost.

4.12 — Code Review: What to Look For, By Risk

Many incidents begin as a pull request that a reviewer approved in ninety seconds. Code review is the practice of having another engineer read a change before it merges. It has two jobs: catch defects early (cheaper than in production), and spread knowledge so no one person is the only one who understands a part of the system. The problem is that reviews are done under time pressure, and the human eye is drawn to what is easy to see: formatting, naming, style. Those matter little compared with a missing timeout or an unchecked permission. A senior reviewer spends attention by risk.

A useful order of attention, from most to least important: (1) Does it do the right thing? Does it solve the actual problem, and is it the right change at all? (2) Safety and security: input validation, authentication and authorization, secrets, injection, data exposure. (3) Failure behaviour: timeouts, retries, error handling, what happens when a dependency is slow or down, idempotency, and race conditions. (4) Data and migrations: irreversible changes, backfills, locking, backwards compatibility. (5) Operability: logging, metrics, alerts, feature flags, rollback path. (6) Tests: do they exercise the risky paths, not just the happy one? (7) Readability and design. (8) Style, ideally left to a formatter or linter so humans never argue about it.

Spend review attention where the damage is
 risk of harm  HIGH  |  1 correctness / 2 security / 3 failure handling / 4 data migrations
                      |  5 operability (logs, metrics, flags, rollback)
                      |  6 tests of risky paths
               LOW   |  7 readability   8 style  <- automate with formatter + linter
 rule of thumb: the further a change is from a tested, reversible path, the slower you read
Size the review by the risk of the change

Not every change deserves the same care. Renaming a variable in a test file and changing how payments are captured are both "small diffs". Risk depends on what is touched, not only on how many lines. Areas like authentication, payments, migrations, infrastructure config, and concurrency deserve a slower read and possibly two reviewers. Very large changes are hard to review; the usual advice is to keep pull requests under a few hundred lines so that a reviewer can hold the whole thing in their head. The block turns this into a simple risk score that could route reviews to the right depth.

A change-risk score to route the review

(2, 'normal review') (5, 'careful review') (10, 'deep review + 2 reviewers')

The one-line config change scores as "careful review" even though it is only six lines, because it lives in a risky area and has no tests. That is exactly the shape of our worked incident: small diff, big blast radius. A reviewer who saw only "6 lines changed" would have skimmed it.

A review checklist you can actually use

Checklists work because they move attention from memory to paper. Keep yours short enough to use every time. For each change ask: What is the purpose, and is there a linked issue? What is the worst thing that could happen if this is wrong, and how would we notice? What happens on failure, timeout, empty input, duplicate request? Are secrets, personal data or permissions involved? Is the change reversible, and can it be turned off? Is there a test that fails without this change? Will the logs and metrics show whether it works? Does it match how the rest of the code is written? A checklist is a floor, not a ceiling: after it, think about what this specific change could break.

[ ] Failure paths: timeout, retry, empty/null input, duplicates, partial failure [ ] Security: input validated, authz checked, no secrets or personal data in logs [ ] Data: migration reversible, backwards compatible, safe on a big table [ ] Tests fail without the change and cover the risky path [ ] Observability: log/metric/alert tells us if it breaks
Giving and receiving feedback

Review comments are a written conversation, and text carries less warmth than voice. A few habits keep them useful. Comment on the code, not the person: say "this query has no timeout" rather than "you forgot the timeout". Label severity so the author knows what blocks the merge: blocking (must fix), suggestion (consider), nit (tiny, take it or leave it), question (I do not understand; help me). Explain the why and offer an alternative when you can. Praise good work specifically; it teaches what to repeat. As an author, assume good intent, keep pull requests small, describe what and why in the description, and reply to every comment: agree and fix, or disagree with a reason. Disagreement is healthy; a comment war is a sign to talk on a call. Both sides should aim to finish a review within one working day, since slow reviews teach people to batch giant changes.

4.13 — Spotting Seeded Defects: Build a Diff Checker

Human reviewers are good at judging design and poor at noticing the same small mistakes for the hundredth time. The mistakes that show up again and again in incident reports are few: a hard-coded secret, a network call with no timeout, an SQL query built by string formatting, an exception swallowed with pass, a test deleted to make the build green. Machines are excellent at spotting fixed patterns, so let them do it and free the human for the questions only a person can answer. In this section you will build the core of such a tool, a diff checker, step by step. It is a small cousin of the linters and static analyzers that run in real pull-request pipelines.

First, what does a diff look like? A unified diff shows changes between two versions of a file. Lines starting with + were added, lines with - were removed, and lines starting with a space are unchanged context. Each group of changes (a hunk) begins with a header @@ -10,6 +10,12 @@, which says the hunk starts at line 10 of the old file and line 10 of the new one. To report "line 15 of orders.py", the checker must track the new-file line number as it walks the diff: context and added lines advance it, removed lines do not.

Step 1: walk a diff and keep the added lines with their line numbers
import re DIFF = """--- a/app/orders.py +++ b/app/orders.py @@ -10,3 +10,5 @@ def create(order): total = order.total + r = requests.get(URL) + print("debug", total) return total """ path, lineno, added = None, 0, [] for line in DIFF.splitlines(): if line.startswith("+++ b/"): path = line[6:] elif line.startswith("@@"): lineno = int(re.search(r"\+(\d+)", line).group(1)) - 1 elif line.startswith("+") and not line.startswith("+++"): lineno += 1; added.append((path, lineno, line[1:].strip())) elif line.startswith(" "): lineno += 1 for item in added: print(item)
('app/orders.py', 11, 'r = requests.get(URL)') ('app/orders.py', 12, 'print("debug", total)')

What Python does, line by line. The loop looks at each diff line in turn. A +++ b/... line names the new file. A @@ header sets lineno to one before the hunk start (the regex takes the number after the plus sign). Each context line (starts with a space) moves lineno forward; each added line moves it forward too and is recorded with its new line number. The context line total = order.total is line 10, so the two added lines are 11 and 12. The check not line.startswith("+++") stops the file header from being mistaken for an added line, a classic off-by-one bug.

Step 2: rules as data

Now add the rules. Keep them as data (an id, a pattern, a message and a severity) so a new lesson from a new incident is one more table row. Patterns are regular expressions; some need extra logic, such as an except: line followed by pass, which needs the previous added line. Try it on a diff seeded with a handful of defects.

Step 2: a rules table and a checker on a seeded diff
import re RULES = [ ("S1", "high", r"(?i)(api_key|secret|password|token)\s*=\s*[\"'][^\"']{6,}[\"']", "hard-coded secret"), ("S2", "high", r"execute\(\s*f[\"']", "SQL built with an f-string (injection)"), ("R1", "medium", r"requests\.(get|post|put|delete)\((?!.*timeout)", "network call without timeout"), ("R2", "medium", r"^except\s*:", "bare except hides real errors"), ("R3", "low", r"^\s*print\(", "debug print left in"), ] DIFF = """+++ b/app/pay.py @@ -1,2 +1,9 @@ import requests +API_KEY = "sk-demo-4471aa90" +row = db.execute(f"SELECT * FROM users WHERE id = {uid}") +r = requests.post(URL, json=body) +try: + pay(order) +except: + pass """ lineno, prev = 0, "" for line in DIFF.splitlines(): if line.startswith("@@"): lineno = int(re.search(r"\+(\d+)", line).group(1)) - 1 elif line.startswith("+") and not line.startswith("+++"): lineno += 1; code = line[1:] for rid, sev, pat, msg in RULES: if re.search(pat, code): print(f"pay.py:{lineno} [{rid} {sev}] {msg}") if code.strip() == "pass" and prev.startswith("except"): print(f"pay.py:{lineno} [R4 medium] exception swallowed with pass") prev = code elif line.startswith(" "): lineno += 1
pay.py:2 [S1 high] hard-coded secret pay.py:3 [S2 high] SQL built with an f-string (injection) pay.py:4 [R1 medium] network call without timeout pay.py:7 [R2 medium] bare except hides real errors pay.py:8 [R4 medium] exception swallowed with pass

The checker flags all five seeded problems with exact line numbers. Notice what it did not say: it does not claim the diff is wrong overall, only that each finding deserves a human look. A tool like this should be tuned for few false positives. If it cries wolf, people learn to ignore it, exactly as with noisy alerts. That is why each rule is narrow: the secret rule needs a quoted value of at least six characters, and the timeout rule uses a negative lookahead (?!.*timeout), which means "not followed by the word timeout on the same line".

Step 3: test the rules themselves

A rule is code, and code needs tests. For each rule keep a few lines that must match and a few that must not. When a real incident teaches you a new pattern, add its example. When someone complains of a false positive, add that line as a "must not match" and fix the pattern. This is the same regression habit from 4.11, applied to your tools.

Step 3: must-match and must-not-match cases for two rules
import re RULES = { "R1 no timeout": r"requests\.(get|post|put|delete)\((?!.*timeout)", "S1 secret": r"(?i)(api_key|secret|password|token)\s*=\s*[\"'][^\"']{6,}[\"']", } CASES = [ ("R1 no timeout", 'r = requests.get(URL)', True), ("R1 no timeout", 'r = requests.get(URL, timeout=2)', False), ("S1 secret", 'API_KEY = "sk-demo-4471aa90"', True), ("S1 secret", 'API_KEY = os.environ["API_KEY"]', False), ("S1 secret", 'password = ""', False), ] for rule, line, expected in CASES: got = bool(re.search(RULES[rule], line)) print("PASS" if got == expected else "FAIL", rule, "|", line)
PASS R1 no timeout | r = requests.get(URL) PASS R1 no timeout | r = requests.get(URL, timeout=2) PASS S1 secret | API_KEY = "sk-demo-4471aa90" PASS S1 secret | API_KEY = os.environ["API_KEY"] PASS S1 secret | password = ""

The os.environ[...] line is a must-not-match case: reading a secret from the environment is the right way, and a checker that flagged it would punish good behaviour. Design rules from both sides. The practice tab has a graded exercise in which you write a checker like this for a new diff; the defect list is different, so you will need to write your own rules rather than copy these.

💡
What a machine cannot see. This checker finds patterns, not meaning. It cannot tell you that the change solves the wrong problem, that a permission check is on the wrong object, or that two requests can interleave and double-charge. Those need a human, which is why the checker is a helper for review and never a replacement for it.
⚠️
Common mistakes. (1) Reporting the line number of the old file instead of the new one, so comments land on the wrong line. (2) Scanning removed and context lines as if they were added, which reports old problems as new. (3) A greedy pattern that matches inside comments or strings. (4) Making the tool block merges on low-severity findings; use severity so only high findings block.
Check yourself: In a diff, which lines should a "hard-coded secret" rule scan, and why?
Only added lines (starting with +). Removed lines are gone, and context lines are old code that this change did not introduce.
Check yourself: What does the regex (?!.*timeout) mean in the network-call rule?
A negative lookahead: match only if the rest of the line does NOT contain "timeout". It allows calls that already set a timeout.

Try it. Add a rule ("R5", "medium", r"verify\s*=\s*False", "TLS verification disabled") to step 2, add a matching line to the diff, and confirm it fires. Then add a "must not match" case to step 3 for verify=True.

Recap
  • A unified diff has files, hunk headers with line numbers, and lines marked +, - or space.
  • Scan only added lines, and track the new-file line number correctly.
  • Keep rules as data with an id, pattern, message and severity; keep patterns narrow to avoid false positives.
  • Test the rules with must-match and must-not-match lines; a checker helps review but never replaces it.
4.14 — Cost and Reliability Trade-offs

Everything in this chapter costs something. Redundant servers cost money. Canaries slow releases. Extra alerts cost sleep. Two reviewers cost time. Postmortems cost hours. A senior engineer does not say "we need more reliability" as if it were free; they say what to buy, how much it costs, and what it saves. The question is never "is reliability good?" but "is this particular improvement worth its price, right now, compared with other things we could do?". This is where the error budget from 4.1 shows its value: it converts reliability into a currency.

Start with how redundancy really behaves. Components in series (a request needs every one of them) multiply their availabilities, so the whole is less reliable than any part: three 99.9% services in a chain give about 99.7%. Components in parallel (any one is enough) multiply their failure chances: two independent 99% servers behind a load balancer fail together only 0.01% of the time, giving 99.99%. The catch is "independent". Two servers on the same power supply, same deploy or same bad config fail together, which is why the pool incident hit all replicas at once: the failure was in the configuration, not the hardware.

Series versus parallel availability, and what it costs
def series(*avail): r = 1.0 for a in avail: r *= a return r def parallel(*avail): fail = 1.0 for a in avail: fail *= (1 - a) return 1 - fail print(f"3 services in a chain, each 99.9% -> {series(.999, .999, .999):.4%}") print(f"1 server at 99% -> {parallel(.99):.4%} cost x1") print(f"2 servers in parallel -> {parallel(.99, .99):.4%} cost x2") print(f"3 servers in parallel -> {parallel(.99, .99, .99):.4%} cost x3") print(f"2 servers + shared bad config (99.5%)-> {series(parallel(.99, .99), .995):.4%}")
3 services in a chain, each 99.9% -> 99.7003% 1 server at 99% -> 99.0000% cost x1 2 servers in parallel -> 99.9900% cost x2 3 servers in parallel -> 99.9999% cost x3 2 servers + shared bad config (99.5%)-> 99.4900%

The last line is the important one. Adding a second server took availability from 99% to 99.99%, but as soon as a shared single point of failure (a config, a database, a deploy pipeline) is in series with it, the total is capped near the weakest link, 99.5% here. Money spent on more replicas cannot fix a shared cause. That is why the postmortem's most valuable actions were about config validation and canaries, not more servers.

Choosing what to buy: expected loss versus cost

To compare options, estimate for each: the expected yearly loss it avoids (chance per year times cost per incident), and its yearly cost (money plus engineering time). Rank by benefit per unit cost. The numbers are rough, but even rough numbers beat arguing by instinct. Include hidden costs: each extra alert adds on-call load, each extra approval adds delay to every future change. Include hidden benefits too: a fix that shortens all incidents pays back many times.

Rank prevention options by yearly benefit per cost
# incident: ~4 per year at a cost of 6,000 each (lost orders + engineer time) = 24,000 of yearly loss options = [ # (name, share_of_loss_avoided, yearly_cost) ("config validator in CI", 0.35, 1_500), ("canary stage for config", 0.30, 4_000), ("pool saturation alert", 0.15, 500), ("second region, active-active", 0.40, 30_000), ] yearly_loss = 4 * 6000 for name, share, cost in sorted(options, key=lambda o: -(o[1] * yearly_loss / o[2])): benefit = share * yearly_loss verdict = "worth it" if benefit > cost else "not now" print(f"{name:<30} avoids {benefit:>6,.0f}/yr for {cost:>6,}/yr -> ratio {benefit/cost:>5.1f} {verdict}")
pool saturation alert avoids 3,600/yr for 500/yr -> ratio 7.2 worth it config validator in CI avoids 8,400/yr for 1,500/yr -> ratio 5.6 worth it canary stage for config avoids 7,200/yr for 4,000/yr -> ratio 1.8 worth it second region, active-active avoids 9,600/yr for 30,000/yr -> ratio 0.3 not now

The cheap items win by a wide margin, and the second region loses: it avoids a lot but costs more than the whole loss it prevents. That does not mean multi-region is wrong forever. If the business grows and an outage costs 100,000 instead of 6,000, the ratio flips. The decision is a function of your numbers today, and it should be revisited when they change. Do not put every hour into prevention, and do not put none. The healthy pattern is to let the error budget steer: when budget is plentiful, ship faster and take reasonable risks; when it is nearly spent (like the 90% in our incident), shift effort to reliability until it recovers. Also count toil, repetitive manual work that scales with the service (restarting things, hand-editing configs): it is a hidden cost that grows quietly, and the automation ladder from 4.7 is how you pay it down. Finally, decide by the user journey. A checkout deserves a tight SLO because failure loses money and trust; a weekly export can be looser. Uniform gold-plating wastes money where it does not matter.

⚠️
Common mistakes. (1) Buying redundancy while a single shared cause remains. (2) Counting only the price of prevention and not the price of the incident, or vice versa. (3) Treating the numbers as exact: they are estimates; look for decisions that win across a range. (4) Adding controls (approvals, alerts) without ever removing old ones, so the process only grows.
Check yourself: Three 99.9% services are called in sequence for every request. What is the approximate availability of the chain?
About 99.7% (0.999 x 0.999 x 0.999). In series, each dependency lowers the whole.
Check yourself: Why is a second region often a poor first reliability purchase?
It doubles infrastructure cost and complexity and does not protect against shared causes such as bad config or a bad deploy, which cause most incidents. Cheaper prevention (validators, canaries, alerts) usually has a far better benefit-to-cost ratio.

Recap

  • Series multiplies availabilities (worse), parallel multiplies failure chances (better) but only when failures are independent.
  • Shared causes such as config, deploys and databases cap every replica; fix them first.
  • Let the error budget steer the balance between features and reliability, and decide per user journey.
