Senior Engineering 4:
Incidents, Postmortems & Code Review

Systems fail. What separates a senior engineer is not that their code never breaks, it is what happens in the first ten minutes afterwards and in the week that follows. This chapter teaches the whole loop: how to decide how bad something is, how to stop the bleeding before you understand it, how to debug with a method instead of a hunch, how to write a postmortem that changes the system rather than blaming a person, and how to review code so fewer incidents happen at all. Almost every idea has a small runnable exercise on realistic, made-up logs, metrics and diffs.

4.1 — How Bad Is It? Severity, SLIs, SLOs and Error Budgets

Meet the running example for this whole chapter: Pebble Market, a small online shop. Its most important feature is POST /checkout, the request that turns a basket into an order. On a Tuesday afternoon Asha, who is on call, sees two messages at once. One says the footer of the About page has a typo. The other says some customers get an error page when they pay. Both messages say "urgent". Which one do you drop everything for? Without a shared way to answer, teams fail in two directions: they wake six people at 3 a.m. for a typo, or they let a payment failure sit until Monday because nobody was sure it counted.

The fix is to agree on vocabulary before the emergency. Three terms do most of the work. An SLI (service level indicator) is a measurement of how well the service is doing from the user's side, usually a ratio: good events divided by valid events. "Checkouts that returned a non-error response within 800 ms, divided by all checkouts" is an SLI. An SLO (service level objective) is the target you choose for that indicator over a window of time, for example "99.9% of checkouts are good over 30 days". An SLA (service level agreement) is a promise to a customer with consequences attached, such as a refund. Think of driving: the SLI is your speedometer reading, the SLO is the speed you told yourself you would keep, and the SLA is the ticket you get if you break the posted limit. Engineers set SLOs; lawyers and sales teams set SLAs, and the SLA should always be looser than the SLO so you notice trouble before you owe money.

The error budget: permission to fail, in numbers

If the SLO is 99.9%, then 0.1% of events are allowed to be bad. That allowance is the error budget. It turns a vague wish ("be reliable") into a number you can spend. Shipping a risky feature spends budget if it breaks something. A quiet month leaves budget unspent, which is a sign you could ship faster. Why not aim for 100%? Because each extra nine costs roughly ten times more, users cannot tell the difference once their own phone and network fail more often than you do, and a service that never changes is a service that never improves. The first block turns the percentage into minutes so the numbers feel real.

Allowed downtime per 30 days for each target
MINUTES = 30 * 24 * 60 # 43,200 minutes in a 30-day window for slo in (99.0, 99.5, 99.9, 99.95, 99.99): budget_fraction = 1 - slo / 100 minutes = MINUTES * budget_fraction print(f"SLO {slo:>5}% -> budget {budget_fraction:.4%} -> {minutes:7.1f} min of full outage")
SLO 99.0% -> budget 1.0000% -> 432.0 min of full outage SLO 99.5% -> budget 0.5000% -> 216.0 min of full outage SLO 99.9% -> budget 0.1000% -> 43.2 min of full outage SLO 99.95% -> budget 0.0500% -> 21.6 min of full outage SLO 99.99% -> budget 0.0100% -> 4.3 min of full outage

What Python does, line by line. (1) MINUTES is the length of the window in minutes. (2) The loop visits five candidate targets. (3) 1 - slo/100 converts a percentage into the fraction that is allowed to fail. (4) Multiplying by the window gives minutes. Notice that going from 99.9% to 99.99% shrinks the budget from 43.2 minutes to 4.3 minutes: less time than a typical deploy takes to roll back. That is why the fourth nine is a serious engineering commitment, not a rounding choice.

Spending the budget: consumption and burn rate

Time is only one way to count. Most services measure requests instead, because a one-minute outage at 3 a.m. hurts far fewer people than one at lunch. Suppose Pebble Market served 10,000,000 checkout requests this month and the SLO is 99.9%. The budget is 10,000 bad requests. If 4,200 have failed so far, 42% of the budget is gone. The number that matters most during an incident is the burn rate: how fast you are spending relative to the rate that would exactly use up the budget at the end of the window. A burn rate of 1 means you will land on zero at the deadline. A burn rate of 10 means the whole month's budget is gone in three days. Burn rate is what alerts should watch, because it says both "something is wrong" and "how urgent it is" in one number.

Consumption and burn rate
def budget_report(slo, window_requests, bad, window_h, elapsed_h): allowed = window_requests * (1 - slo) # bad requests the whole window may contain consumed = bad / allowed # share of the budget already spent burn = consumed / (elapsed_h / window_h) # 1.0 == spending exactly on plan hours_left = (1 - consumed) / (consumed / elapsed_h) return allowed, consumed, burn, hours_left # One hour of a bad deploy: 300 failures at 50,000 requests/hour (36M in a 720 h month) allowed, consumed, burn, left = budget_report(0.999, 36_000_000, 300, 720, 1) print(f"one bad hour: {consumed:.1%} of the month's budget, burn rate {burn:.1f}x") # Whole month is 10M requests; 4,200 bad so far, 240 hours in allowed, consumed, burn, left = budget_report(0.999, 10_000_000, 4200, 720, 240) print(f"month so far: {consumed:.0%} consumed, burn {burn:.2f}x, empty in ~{left:.0f} more hours")
one bad hour: 0.8% of the month's budget, burn rate 6.0x month so far: 42% consumed, burn 1.26x, empty in ~331 more hours

Read the first line of output carefully: 300 failures in an hour looks small, but it burns 0.8% of the whole month's budget in one hour, which is six times the pace the plan allows. In the second scenario one third of the month has passed and 42% of the budget is gone, a burn rate of 1.26x: at this pace the budget runs out in about 331 more hours, before the 720-hour window closes. Numbers like these are what let a team say "we should stop shipping features and fix reliability" without a shouting match. Many teams write that into an error budget policy: above 100% consumed, feature work pauses until the budget recovers or the causes are fixed.

Severity levels: sorting by impact, not by noise

A severity level is a label that says how bad an incident is and therefore how loudly to respond. The key rule: severity is decided by customer impact, never by how scary the error message looks or how senior the person shouting is. A typical ladder has four rungs. SEV1: a core function is down for many users, data is being lost, or customer data is exposed; page people immediately and give updates every 30 minutes. SEV2: a core function is badly degraded or one important segment is down; page the on-call and update hourly. SEV3: a minor feature is broken or a workaround exists; handle in working hours. SEV4: cosmetic or internal-only; put it in the backlog. You can raise or lower severity as facts arrive, and you should. Starting high and downgrading is cheap. Starting low and discovering an hour later that it was a SEV1 is not.

A first severity classifier
def severity(pct_users_hit, core_path, data_loss, workaround): if data_loss or (core_path and pct_users_hit >= 25): return "SEV1" if core_path and pct_users_hit >= 5: return "SEV2" if pct_users_hit >= 5 and not workaround: return "SEV2" if pct_users_hit > 0 and not workaround: return "SEV3" return "SEV4" print("checkout fails for 12% of users :", severity(12, True, False, False)) print("orders silently dropped :", severity(1, True, True, False)) print("export button fails for 2% :", severity(2, False, False, False)) print("About-page typo :", severity(0, False, False, True))
checkout fails for 12% of users : SEV2 orders silently dropped : SEV1 export button fails for 2% : SEV3 About-page typo : SEV4
Severity ladder and what each rung triggers
SEV1  core path down for many / data loss / breach
      -> page now, incident commander, all-hands, updates every 30 min
SEV2  core path degraded / big segment down
      -> page on-call, dedicated channel, updates every 60 min
SEV3  minor break, workaround exists
      -> ticket, fix in working hours, no page
SEV4  cosmetic, internal only
      -> backlog
⚠️
Common mistakes with SLOs. (1) Measuring the server instead of the user: a service that answers 200 with an empty basket is "up" on a dashboard and broken for the customer. (2) Choosing 100%: it makes every deploy a violation. (3) One SLO for everything: checkout deserves 99.9%, a nightly report might be fine at 99%. (4) Setting severity by feeling. Write the rules down, like the classifier above, and argue about the rules in daylight, not at 3 a.m.
Check yourself: A month has 43.2 minutes of budget at 99.9%. A 30-minute full outage happens on day 3. What fraction is spent, and what does that mean for the rest of the month?
30 / 43.2 is about 69% spent in the first 10% of the window. The burn rate for the month is roughly 7x, so the team is in a "slow down and fix reliability first" position.
Check yourself: Why should the SLA be looser than the SLO?
The SLO is your early-warning line. If you breach it you notice and fix things while customers still have not been owed anything. If both were equal you would only find out at the moment you start paying refunds.

Try it. Change the SLO in the first block to 99.97 and predict the minutes before you run it. Then change severity() so that data loss alone is SEV1 only when more than one record is lost, and think about whether that is a good rule.

Recap
  • An SLI measures user experience as good events over valid events; an SLO is the target; an SLA is a contract with penalties.
  • The error budget is 1 minus the SLO. It makes reliability a number you can spend.
  • Burn rate (observed error rate divided by allowed rate) tells you how urgent a problem is.
  • Severity comes from customer impact and rules written in advance; adjust it as facts arrive.
