1.1 — The Framework: Decide Before You Build

The problem

Asha is asked to "build a photo-sharing service". She opens her editor and starts with the database schema, because tables feel concrete. Three weeks later the team learns that users upload 20 million photos a day, that most traffic is people scrolling other people's photos, and that the storage bill is the real product risk. The schema was fine. The decisions around it were never made.

Mid-level engineers are usually rewarded for solving the problem in front of them. Senior engineers are rewarded for choosing which problem is in front of everyone, and for showing why the chosen design will survive contact with real traffic. System design is the discipline of making those choices on paper, cheaply, before code makes them expensive to change.

The idea: one framework, nine questions

Think of a civil engineer designing a bridge. She does not start by welding steel. She asks who will cross it, how heavy the loads are, what the river does in a flood, and what happens if one cable snaps. Only then does she draw the structure. A software design has the same shape. We use nine steps, always in this order, and each step feeds the next.

The nine-step design framework
 1 REQUIREMENTS ──► 2 CONSTRAINTS ──► 3 ESTIMATION
   what & for whom     time, money,       QPS, storage,
                       team, SLOs         bandwidth

   DESIGN            entities, keys    contract

     ▼

   the 1-2 hard      what breaks,        what we chose,
   components        how we notice       what we gave up

Here is what each step is for, in plain words.

  • Requirements: what the system must do (functional) and how well (non-functional: speed, uptime, correctness). Ask until you can write a one-paragraph problem statement.
  • Constraints: the fixed walls. A team of three, a launch in eight weeks, data that must stay in one country. Constraints eliminate options early, which is a gift.
  • Estimation: rough numbers for requests per second, storage, and network. This tells you whether one machine is enough or you need a fleet.
  • API: the promises the system makes to callers. Writing it forces you to name the nouns and the operations.
  • Data model: what you store, how it is keyed, and which queries must be fast.
  • High-level design: boxes and arrows. Enough to point at and argue about.
  • Deep dives: take the one or two riskiest boxes and design them properly.
  • Failure modes: for every box, what happens when it is slow, down, or wrong?
  • Trade-offs: say out loud what you chose, what you rejected, and what the choice costs.

The order matters because each step removes options for the next. Estimation before design tells you if you need sharding at all. Failure modes after deep dives means you are testing a real design, not a slogan.

Recap

  • Nine steps in order: requirements, constraints, estimation, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, failure modes, trade-offs.

  • The framework scales with risk: minutes for a small tool, pages for payments.
1.2 — Requirements and Constraints: Turn Adjectives into Numbers

The problem

A product manager says: "The link service has to be fast, reliable, and scale." Ben nods, builds it, and ships. Two weeks later there is an argument. Is a 900 ms redirect fast? Is one outage of forty minutes in a month acceptable? Nobody can answer, because "fast" and "reliable" are feelings, not requirements. A design cannot be judged against a feeling.

The idea: functional, non-functional, and the three S-words

Functional requirements say what the system does: "a user can create a short link; anyone can follow it and is redirected." Non-functional requirements say how well: speed, uptime, durability, consistency, cost, privacy. Non-functional requirements are what actually shape the architecture, because two systems with identical features can need completely different designs.

Three terms turn adjectives into numbers:

- An SLI (service level indicator) is a measurement: "the fraction of redirects answered in under 100 ms." - An SLO (service level objective) is your internal target for that measurement: "99% of redirects under 100 ms, measured over 30 days." - An SLA (service level agreement) is a promise to a customer with consequences, usually money: "below 99.9% uptime we refund 10%." Set your SLO stricter than your SLA so you notice trouble before you owe anyone anything.

Think of a restaurant. The SLI is how long the food actually took. The SLO is "we aim for 15 minutes". The SLA is "over 30 minutes and dessert is free".

Availability in nines

Uptime is quoted in "nines". The arithmetic is short enough to do in your head once you have seen it.

Downtime allowed per number of nines
MIN_PER_MONTH = 30 * 24 * 60 # 43,200 minutes for name, avail in [("two nines", 0.99), ("three nines", 0.999), ("four nines", 0.9999), ("five nines", 0.99999)]: budget_min = MIN_PER_MONTH * (1 - avail) print(f"{name:<12} {avail:<8} -> {budget_min:8.2f} min/month " f"({budget_min * 12 / 60:6.2f} h/year)")
two nines 0.99 -> 432.00 min/month ( 86.40 h/year) three nines 0.999 -> 43.20 min/month ( 8.64 h/year) four nines 0.9999 -> 4.32 min/month ( 0.86 h/year) five nines 0.99999 -> 0.43 min/month ( 0.09 h/year)

That allowed downtime is your error budget: the amount of failure you may spend on deployments, experiments, and bad luck. At three nines you have about 43 minutes a month. One slow, careless deploy can burn most of it. At five nines you have 26 seconds a month, which no human can respond to. Five nines therefore means fully automatic failover, and that costs a lot of money and design effort.

Availability composes, and mostly downward

Suppose your service needs an app server (99.9%), a database (99.9%) and a payment provider (99.9%) all to be up for a request to succeed. The chance that all three are up is the product.

Dependencies multiply, redundancy helps
def serial(*avails): total = 1.0 for a in avails: total *= a return total def parallel(a, n): return 1 - (1 - a) ** n # at least one of n copies is up chain = serial(0.999, 0.999, 0.999) print(f"3 serial 99.9% deps -> {chain:.5f} ({(1 - chain) * 43200:.1f} min/month down)") print(f"2 replicas of 99.9% -> {parallel(0.999, 2):.6f}") print(f"3 replicas of 99.9% -> {parallel(0.999, 3):.9f}") print(f"app x replicas(2) db x replicas(2) -> {serial(parallel(0.999,2), parallel(0.999,2)):.6f}")
3 serial 99.9% deps -> 0.99700 (129.5 min/month down) 2 replicas of 99.9% -> 0.999999 3 replicas of 99.9% -> 0.999999999 app x replicas(2) db x replicas(2) -> 0.999998
What this shows. Three dependencies each at 99.9% give roughly 99.7% overall, about three times the downtime of than any single one. Adding a second replica of a component turns 99.9% into 99.9999%, provided failures are independent. That proviso is the catch: two replicas in the same rack, on the same power feed, running the same buggy release, fail together. Real redundancy needs separation (different zones) and staggered releases.

Trade-offs of aiming higher

TargetWhat it typically demandsCost of chasing it
99% (two nines)Single instance, manual restartCheap; users notice outages
99.9% (three)Redundant instances, health checks, on-callModerate; needs monitoring
99.99% (four)Multi-zone, automatic failover, careful deploysHigh; slows feature work
99.999% (five)Multi-region, no human in the loopVery high; rarely justified

Questions that expose hidden requirements

Before drawing anything, ask questions whose answers change the design:

- Who are the users and roughly how many? Is traffic steady or spiky (a sale, a live event)? - Is it read-heavy or write-heavy? What is the ratio? - What happens if data is stale for a few seconds? For an hour? (This decides the consistency model.) - What happens if we lose data? (Durability: a like count and a bank balance are not the same.) - What is out of scope for version 1? Write it down; non-goals protect your schedule.

⚠️
Common mistake: promising more nines than your dependencies allowIf your service calls a partner API that only offers 99.5%, your best possible availability on the paths that need it is below 99.5%, no matter how good your code is. Either make that call optional (degrade gracefully), cache the answer, or lower your promise.
Check yourself: An SLA promises 99.95% monthly uptime. Roughly how many minutes of downtime are allowed in a 30-day month?
43,200 minutes times 0.0005 is 21.6 minutes. That is the whole budget for outages and for risky deployments combined.
Recap
  • Non-functional requirements shape architecture; write them as measurable SLIs and SLOs.
  • Keep the SLO stricter than the SLA so you get warning before penalties.
  • Serial dependencies multiply availability downward; independent replicas raise it upward.
  • Ask about read/write ratio, staleness, data loss, and non-goals before drawing boxes.
1.3 — Back-of-Envelope Estimation: Sizing Before Designing

The problem

Chen designs a notification system with a single Postgres table and a cron job. It works in testing with 1,000 users. At launch there are 40 million users, the cron job takes 31 hours to finish a 24-hour cycle, and the table is 2 TB. A five-minute calculation before design would have shown that the "simple" design was off by a factor of a hundred. Back-of-envelope estimation means computing rough sizes quickly, to within a factor of two or so, to decide which designs are even possible.

The idea: powers of ten, three quantities

You only need three quantities per feature: QPS (queries per second: how often things happen), storage (how many bytes we keep), and bandwidth (bytes per second on the wire). Everything comes from a handful of tricks.

- There are 86,400 seconds in a day. Round it to about 100,000 (105). "Ten million events per day" is then about 100 per second. Use the precise number when it matters. - Peak traffic is higher than average. Multiply average by 2 to 5 (use 3 if nothing better is known) because people are asleep half the day. - Storage = records per day x bytes per record x days kept x replication factor (typically 3) x index/overhead (typically 1.3 to 2). - Bandwidth = QPS x average payload size. Bits versus bytes: network links are quoted in bits per second, disks and payloads in bytes. Divide bits by 8. - Convert units by counting zeros: 1 KB = 103, MB = 106, GB = 109, TB = 1012 bytes.

Think of it as packing for a trip. You do not weigh each sock. You know a suitcase holds about 20 kg and a shirt is about 200 g, and that is enough to decide whether you need one bag or three.

Latency numbers worth remembering

Speed differences between memory, disk and network are why architecture looks the way it does. These are rough, current orders of magnitude.

Check yourself: 300 million requests per day is roughly how many per second on average, and what would you plan for at peak?

Recap
  • Estimate QPS, storage, and bandwidth; use 86,400 s per day and a peak factor of about 3.
  • Storage = records x size x retention x replication x overhead; watch bits versus bytes.
  • Memory is about 1000x faster than a network hop; that ratio drives caching and batching.
  • Always check the failure case: capacity after losing a zone or a server.
1.4 — Caching: Trading Freshness for Speed

The problem

Dana's product page reads from a database. At 400 requests per second it answers in 8 ms. On sale day traffic reaches 5,000 per second; the database saturates, queries queue, and the same page takes two seconds. Nearly all those requests ask for the same few hundred popular products. The database is repeating identical work thousands of times a minute.

The idea: keep a copy of hot answers somewhere faster

A cache is a small, fast store that holds copies of answers so you can skip the slow source. It works because access is uneven: a few items get most of the traffic. This property is called locality. Think of your kitchen. Salt lives on the counter (cache), flour in the pantry (database), and everything else at the supermarket (slow origin). You keep on the counter what you reach for most.

Caching is never free. The copy can become out of date, so every cache is a trade: speed for freshness. The whole art is deciding how stale is acceptable and how to control it.

Smallest example: cache-aside

The most common pattern is cache-aside (also called lazy loading). The application, not the cache, is in charge: look in the cache; on a miss, read the database, then store the result.

Cache-aside, and the stale-data trap
db = {"p1": "Kettle", "p2": "Toaster"} cache, db_calls = {}, 0 def get(key): global db_calls if key in cache: return cache[key] # hit: no database work db_calls += 1 cache[key] = db[key] # miss: load and remember return cache[key] for k in ["p1", "p1", "p2", "p1", "p2"]: get(k) print("db calls for 5 reads:", db_calls) db["p1"] = "Kettle v2" # someone updates the source print("after update, cached read:", get("p1")) # stale! cache.pop("p1", None) # invalidate on write print("after invalidation: ", get("p1"))
db calls for 5 reads: 2 after update, cached read: Kettle after invalidation: Kettle v2
Trace. The first read of each key misses (2 database calls); the other three reads are hits. After the database changes, the cache still returns the old name: this is stale data. Deleting the key on write (invalidation) fixes it, because the next read misses and reloads. Notice we delete rather than overwrite: deleting is safer when two writers race, because the next reader always reloads the newest truth.

How much does a cache actually help?

The average latency is a weighted mix: h x t_cache + (1 - h) x t_db, where h is the hit rate. Because misses are so much slower, small hit-rate gains near the top are worth a lot.

Hit rate versus average latency
t_cache, t_db = 1.0, 20.0 # milliseconds for h in (0.0, 0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99): avg = h * t_cache + (1 - h) * t_db db_qps = 5000 * (1 - h) # what the database still has to serve print(f"hit rate {h:4.0%}: avg {avg:5.2f} ms, database sees {db_qps:6.0f} req/s")
hit rate 0%: avg 20.00 ms, database sees 5000 req/s hit rate 50%: avg 10.50 ms, database sees 2500 req/s hit rate 80%: avg 4.80 ms, database sees 1000 req/s hit rate 90%: avg 2.90 ms, database sees 500 req/s hit rate 95%: avg 1.95 ms, database sees 250 req/s hit rate 99%: avg 1.19 ms, database sees 50 req/s

Going from 90% to 99% cuts average latency from 2.9 ms to 1.2 ms, but the more important number is the last column: the database load falls from 500 to 50 requests per second. A cache is mainly a shield for the database.

Eviction: what to throw away

A cache is smaller than the data, so it must evict. LRU (least recently used) drops the item untouched for longest. LFU (least frequently used) drops the item used least often. TTL (time to live) drops items after a fixed age regardless of use. Run an LRU against a realistic, skewed workload (a few keys are very popular, following a Zipf-like pattern) and watch diminishing returns.

LRU cache hit rate versus capacity
from collections import OrderedDict import bisect def zipf_trace(n_keys, n_req, seed=7): cum, acc = [], 0.0 tot = sum(1 / r for r in range(1, n_keys + 1)) for r in range(1, n_keys + 1): acc += 1 / r / tot; cum.append(acc) s, out = seed, [] for _ in range(n_req): s = (s * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2**31 out.append(bisect.bisect_left(cum, s / 2**31)) return out def lru_hit_rate(trace, cap): d, hits = OrderedDict(), 0 for k in trace: if k in d: d.move_to_end(k); hits += 1 else: d[k] = 1 if len(d) > cap: d.popitem(last=False) return hits / len(trace) trace = zipf_trace(10_000, 50_000) for cap in (10, 100, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000): print(f"cap {cap:>5} ({cap/100:>4.1f}% of keys) hit rate {lru_hit_rate(trace, cap):.3f}")

Caching 1% of the keys already serves 39% of requests; 10% of the keys serve 67%; doubling to 20% only reaches 75%. The curve flattens. That shape is why you size a cache by measuring the working set (the keys actually used in a window) and stop where the curve bends, not where memory runs out.

Here is TTL's trade in numbers. One key is read every tick; the source changes about every 47 ticks.

TTL: fewer database reads, more stale reads
def run_ttl(ttl, ticks=1000, change_every=47): cache_ver, cache_at, db_reads, stale = None, -10**9, 0, 0 for t in range(ticks): current = t // change_every # the true version right now if t - cache_at >= ttl: # expired: reload cache_ver, cache_at = current, t db_reads += 1 if cache_ver != current: stale += 1 return db_reads, stale for ttl in (1, 5, 20, 60): r, s = run_ttl(ttl) print(f"ttl {ttl:>3}: database reads {r:>4}, stale reads {s:>3} of 1000")

A 60-tick TTL cuts database reads from 1,000 to 17 but serves stale data 56% of the time. Whether that is acceptable is a product question: fine for a product description, unacceptable for an account balance.

The stampede

Three ways to survive an expiry
def stampede(strategy, reqs=200, recompute_ms=50): ready_at, db_calls, waited = None, 0, 0 for t in range(reqs): # one request per ms if ready_at is not None and t >= ready_at: continue # value is back: hit if strategy == "naive" or ready_at is None: db_calls += 1 if ready_at is None: ready_at = t + recompute_ms if strategy == "stale_ok": continue # serve the old copy, never wait waited += 1 return db_calls, waited for s in ("naive", "single_flight", "stale_ok"): calls, waited = stampede(s) print(f"{s:<14} database calls {calls:>3}, requests that waited {waited:>3}")

Naive sends one database call per missing request (50 identical queries). Single-flight (a lock or a shared "in progress" marker) sends one; the others wait for it. Stale-while-revalidate sends one and lets everyone else keep reading the old value, so nobody waits. A second, related trap is many keys expiring together, for example a batch loaded at the same second with the same TTL. The fix is jitter: add a random amount to each TTL so expiries spread out.
TTL jitter spreads expiries

1.5 — Databases: Choosing, Indexing, Replicating, Trading Consistency

The problem

Eli's team picks a database because a conference talk was exciting. Six months later the product needs "all orders for a customer, with their items, in one consistent view" and the chosen store cannot join or run multi-record transactions. Another team picks a relational database for a click stream that grows 2 TB a week and watches it choke on writes. In both cases the store was chosen before anyone wrote down the access patterns: which reads and writes will happen, how often, and how correct they must be.

Start from the queries, then pick the shape of storage that answers them cheaply. Here is the map.

Indexes: the book's index for your table

Without an index, finding one row means reading every row (a scan). An index is a sorted side structure (usually a B-tree) that lets the database jump to the row like a book's index. The real database engine SQLite ships with Python, so you can ask it what it would do.

Replication: copies for reads and for survival

One machine is a single point of failure and has a read limit. Replication keeps copies on other machines. The classic setup is one leader that accepts writes and several followers that copy them and serve reads. If the leader dies, a follower is promoted.

Leader and followers with replication lag
   writes ─►  ┌────────┐   async copy (takes time)   ┌───────────┐ ◄─ reads
              │ LEADER │ ───────────────────────────►│ FOLLOWER 1│
              └────────┘ ───────────────────────────►├───────────┤ ◄─ reads
                                                      │ FOLLOWER 2│
                                                      └───────────┘
   A read from a follower can return data older than the last write.

Copying takes time, so followers lag. This causes a classic bug: a user saves a new bio, the page reloads from a follower, and the old bio appears.

Replication lag: stale read, then the fix
class Store: def __init__(self, lag): self.leader, self.follower, self.pending, self.lag, self.t = {}, {}, [], lag, 0 def write(self, k, v): self.leader[k] = v self.pending.append((self.t + self.lag, k, v)) def tick(self): self.t += 1 for due, k, v in [p for p in self.pending if p[0] <= self.t]: self.follower[k] = v self.pending = [p for p in self.pending if p[0] > self.t] def read(self, k, wrote_at=None): # read-your-writes: recent writers read the leader if wrote_at is not None and self.t - wrote_at <= self.lag: return self.leader.get(k) return self.follower.get(k) s = Store(lag=3) s.write("bio", "new bio") for _ in range(5): print(f"t={s.t}: plain follower read {s.follower.get('bio')!r:<10} | routed read {s.read('bio', wrote_at=0)!r}") s.tick()
t=0: plain follower read None | routed read 'new bio' t=1: plain follower read None | routed read 'new bio' t=2: plain follower read None | routed read 'new bio' t=3: plain follower read 'new bio' | routed read 'new bio' t=4: plain follower read 'new bio' | routed read 'new bio'

For three ticks the plain follower read returns None (stale). The routed read is correct because for a short window after a user's own write we send that user to the leader. This is read-your-writes consistency: a user always sees their own changes, even if others briefly do not.

Consistency, CAP and PACELC in plain words

Strong consistency means every read sees the latest write. Eventual consistency means replicas converge if writes stop, but a read may return older data for a while. The CAP idea says: when the network splits the machines into groups that cannot talk (a partition, which will happen), a replicated system must choose. Either it refuses some requests to stay correct (CP: consistent, sacrificing availability on the cut-off side), or it keeps answering with possibly stale data (AP: available, sacrificing consistency). You cannot have both while the split lasts. PACELC adds the everyday case: even with no partition (Else), you choose between low Latency and Consistency, because confirming with more replicas takes longer.

Quorums make this concrete. With N replicas, a write waits for W acknowledgements and a read asks R replicas. If R + W > N, every read set overlaps every write set, so a read always meets at least one replica with the latest value.

⚠️
Common mistakesChoosing a database by popularity instead of access patterns. Adding indexes on everything, then wondering why writes are slow. Reading from followers right after a write without a plan for lag. Assuming "eventual" means "within milliseconds" (it can be minutes during trouble). Believing NoSQL means no schema: the schema simply lives in your application code, where it is harder to enforce.
Check yourself: A payment ledger and a "likes" counter both use replicated storage. Which can tolerate eventual consistency, and why?
The likes counter. A count that is briefly off by a few does no harm and can be repaired later. The ledger must be correct (money moves, audits happen), so it uses strong consistency and accepts higher latency or reduced availability during a partition.
Recap
  • Pick storage from access patterns; default to relational, add specialized stores as derived copies.
  • Indexes turn scans into logarithmic lookups and cost write speed and space.
  • Replication adds read capacity and survival, and introduces lag; use read-your-writes routing.
  • CAP: during a partition choose consistency or availability; PACELC: otherwise choose latency or consistency. R + W > N gives quorum overlap.
1.6 — Partitioning and Sharding: Splitting Data Without Splitting the Team

The problem

A single database server has ceilings: disk size, memory, and how many writes per second it can commit. A read replica helps with reads but every write still lands on the one leader. Farah's team reaches 12,000 writes per second on a machine that tops out near 8,000. Buying a bigger machine works once or twice, then stops (and gets expensive). The remaining option is to split the data across several machines, each owning a slice.

The idea: every key has exactly one home

Sharding (also called horizontal partitioning) splits a data set into shards, each on its own machine, using a shard key such as user_id. A rule maps every key to one shard. Think of a library that splits books across buildings by the first letter of the author's surname. Anyone who knows the rule walks to the right building; nobody searches all of them.

There are three ways to define the rule.

StrategyRuleStrengthsWeaknesses
RangeKeys A-F on shard 1, G-M on shard 2, ...Range scans stay together; easy to understandHot spots when keys arrive in order (timestamps, auto-increment ids)
Hashshard = hash(key) mod NEven spread of keysRange scans hit every shard; changing N moves most keys
DirectoryA lookup table says where each key livesTotal flexibility, easy rebalancingThe directory is a new critical dependency

Why "hash mod N" hurts when N changes

Hash-mod is the first idea everyone has. It has a costly flaw: when you add a shard, N changes and almost every key gets a new home, so nearly all your data has to move at once. Watch it happen.

Adding a shard under hash-mod-N versus a consistent hash ring
import hashlib, bisect def h(s): return int(hashlib.md5(str(s).encode()).hexdigest()[:12], 16) keys = [f"user{i}" for i in range(10_000)] moved = sum(h(k) % 4 != h(k) % 5 for k in keys) print(f"mod-N: 4 -> 5 shards moves {moved} of {len(keys)} keys ({moved/len(keys):.0%})") class Ring: def __init__(self, nodes, vnodes=100): self.ring = sorted((h(f"{n}#{i}"), n) for n in nodes for i in range(vnodes)) self.points = [p for p, _ in self.ring] def owner(self, k): i = bisect.bisect(self.points, h(k)) % len(self.ring) return self.ring[i][1] a = Ring(["A", "B", "C", "D"]); b = Ring(["A", "B", "C", "D", "E"]) moved = sum(a.owner(k) != b.owner(k) for k in keys) print(f"ring: 4 -> 5 nodes moves {moved} of {len(keys)} keys ({moved/len(keys):.0%})")
mod-N: 4 -> 5 shards moves 7968 of 10000 keys (80%) ring: 4 -> 5 nodes moves 1882 of 10000 keys (19%)

Roughly 80% of the keys move with hash-mod-N, against 19% on the ring. The ideal is 20% (one fifth of the data belongs to the new node), so the ring is close to optimal.

Consistent hashing, step by step

Consistent hashing maps both nodes and keys onto the same circle of hash values. A key belongs to the first node you meet walking clockwise from the key's position. When a node joins, it takes over only the arc just before it, and only the keys in that arc move.

What the code does. Ring.__init__ gives every node many virtual nodes (points on the circle named A#0, A#1, ...), sorts all points, and remembers the hash values. owner hashes the key, uses bisect to find the next point clockwise (with % len wrapping past the end), and returns that point's node. Virtual nodes matter for balance:
Virtual nodes even out the load
import hashlib, bisect def h(s): return int(hashlib.md5(str(s).encode()).hexdigest()[:12], 16) keys = [f"user{i}" for i in range(10_000)] def spread(nodes, vnodes): ring = sorted((h(f"{n}#{i}"), n) for n in nodes for i in range(vnodes)) points, counts = [p for p, _ in ring], {} for k in keys: owner = ring[bisect.bisect(points, h(k)) % len(ring)][1] counts[owner] = counts.get(owner, 0) + 1 return counts for vn in (1, 10, 100, 500): c = spread(["A", "B", "C", "D"], vn) print(f"vnodes {vn:>3}: min {min(c.values()):>5} max {max(c.values()):>5} (ideal 2500)")

With one point per node the split is wildly uneven (one node holds 5,631 keys, another 135). With 100 points per node it is within about 4% of ideal. The cost of many virtual nodes is a slightly bigger routing table, which is tiny.

Skew: hashing spreads keys, not traffic

Even a perfect hash cannot fix uneven demand. One celebrity account, one viral product, or one popular hash tag receives far more traffic than average, and whichever shard owns it becomes a hot shard. Range sharding has the opposite problem: keys that grow in order (timestamps, auto-increment ids) all land on the newest range.

Two kinds of skew

The first line shows one shard carrying 10,056 requests, about 1.6 times the average, because the single hottest key is 10% of all traffic and lives on one shard. What sharding costs you

- Cross-shard queries (a join or a global sort) must ask every shard and merge, so they are slow and awkward. Design the shard key so your most common queries touch one shard. - Cross-shard transactions are hard and slow (two-phase commit) or replaced by sagas (a sequence of local steps with compensating undo). Prefer designs where one user's data lives together. - Resharding is an operation you will do under load. Plan for it from day one: virtual nodes, or many small logical shards mapped onto few machines, so you can move a logical shard without rehashing everything. - Unique ids can no longer come from one auto-increment counter; you need an id scheme (see the URL shortener later).

⚠️
Common mistakesSharding too early: a well-indexed single database with replicas is far simpler and carries most products. Choosing a shard key with low cardinality (country, status: a few huge shards). Choosing a shard key you will need to change (moving data between shards is the hardest migration you can face). Forgetting that "fits in memory on one machine" is a legitimate answer.
Check yourself: You shard orders by order_id but the most frequent query is "all orders for one customer". What goes wrong and what would you consider?

Recap
  • Sharding gives each key one home; range, hash and directory strategies trade scans, evenness and flexibility.
  • Hash-mod-N moves almost everything when N changes; a consistent hash ring with virtual nodes moves only about 1/N.
  • Hashing evens out keys, not traffic: hot keys and ordered keys still create hot shards.
  • Choose the shard key by your dominant query, and plan for resharding early.
1.7 — Queues and Async Processing: Delivery, Idempotency, Dead Letters, Backpressure

The problem

When a customer places an order, the API must charge the card, reserve stock, send an email, and update analytics. Done inline, the customer waits for all four; if the email service is slow, checkout is slow; if analytics is down, checkout fails. Gus's team also finds that a traffic spike at 20:00 crushes the payment provider because every request calls it at the same moment. The work does not all need to happen before the reply.

The idea: hand the work to a queue

A message queue is a durable buffer between a producer (who creates work) and a consumer (who does it). The producer writes a message and moves on; consumers take messages at their own pace. Think of a restaurant order rail: the waiter clips the ticket and goes back to the floor, and the kitchen works through tickets as fast as it can. The queue absorbs spikes, decouples services, and lets you scale consumers independently.

Delivery guarantees: at-most-once, at-least-once, "exactly-once"

The consumer must tell the queue it finished (an ack). If the ack never arrives (the worker crashed, the network dropped it), the queue cannot know whether the work was done, so it has two choices.

GuaranteeWhat the queue does when unsureResultUse for
At-most-onceDoes not redeliverMessages can be lost, never duplicatedMetrics, logs where loss is fine

"Exactly-once delivery" over an unreliable network is not achievable; what systems really offer is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing, which has the same effect. An operation is idempotent if doing it twice leaves the same end state as doing it once. Setting a value is idempotent; adding 25 to a balance is not, unless you remember which messages you already applied.

Duplicates from lost acks, and an idempotent consumer
messages = [("m1", 10), ("m2", 25), ("m3", 5), ("m4", 40)] lost_ack = {"m2", "m4"} # the first ack for these gets lost def deliver(handler): acked_once = set() for mid, amount in messages: while True: handler(mid, amount) if mid in lost_ack and mid not in acked_once: acked_once.add(mid) # ack lost: the broker sends it again continue break naive_total = 0 def naive(mid, amt): global naive_total naive_total += amt deliver(naive) seen, safe_total = set(), 0 def idempotent(mid, amt): global safe_total if mid in seen: return # already applied: ignore the duplicate seen.add(mid); safe_total += amt deliver(idempotent) print("expected total:", sum(a for _, a in messages)) print("naive consumer:", naive_total, "| idempotent consumer:", safe_total)

Trace. m2 and m4 are delivered twice because their first ack is "lost". The naive consumer adds 25 and 40 twice, ending at 145 instead of 80: customers charged twice. The idempotent consumer keeps a set of message ids it has applied and skips repeats, so it ends at exactly 80. In production the "seen" set lives in the same database transaction as the effect (or you use a unique constraint), otherwise a crash between the two steps recreates the bug.

Retries, backoff and the dead-letter queue

Some failures are transient (a timeout: try again). Some are poison messages (malformed data that will never succeed). If you retry a poison message forever it blocks the queue. The standard answer: retry a limited number of times with exponential backoff (wait 1 s, 2 s, 4 s, ... up to a cap), then move the message to a dead-letter queue (DLQ) for a human to inspect and replay after a fix.

Bounded retries and a dead-letter queue
MAX_ATTEMPTS = 3 def backoff(attempt, base=1, cap=30): return min(cap, base * 2 ** (attempt - 1)) flaky_fail_first = {"job2": 1, "job4": 2} # transient failures before success poison = {"job3"} # always fails done, dlq, tries = [], [], {} for job in ["job1", "job2", "job3", "job4"]: for attempt in range(1, MAX_ATTEMPTS + 1): tries[job] = attempt fails = job in poison or attempt <= flaky_fail_first.get(job, 0) if not fails: done.append(job); break if attempt == MAX_ATTEMPTS: dlq.append(job) print("backoff delays (s):", [backoff(a) for a in range(1, 7)]) print("done:", done, "| dead-letter queue:", dlq) print("attempts used:", tries)
backoff delays (s): [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 30] done: ['job1', 'job2', 'job4'] | dead-letter queue: ['job3'] attempts used: {'job1': 1, 'job2': 2, 'job3': 3, 'job4': 3}

Transient failures (job2 after 2 tries, job4 after 3) eventually succeed. The poison message job3 uses all three attempts and lands in the DLQ. You must also alert on DLQ depth: a DLQ nobody watches is a silent data-loss machine.

Backpressure: what to do when producers outrun consumers

If work arrives at 120 per second and consumers finish 100 per second, the backlog grows by 20 every second forever. An unbounded queue hides the problem until memory runs out, and every new message waits behind a growing line. Backpressure means pushing the pain back to the source: bound the queue, then either block producers, reject excess (shed load), or scale consumers.

Unbounded versus bounded queue under overload
def simulate(producer_rate, consumer_rate, seconds, limit=None): depth, dropped, rows = 0, 0, [] for t in range(1, seconds + 1): depth += producer_rate if limit is not None and depth > limit: dropped += depth - limit; depth = limit # shed the excess depth = max(0, depth - consumer_rate) rows.append(depth) return rows, dropped, depth / consumer_rate # wait = backlog / service rate for label, lim in (("unbounded", None), ("bounded(300)", 300)): rows, dropped, wait = simulate(120, 100, 30, lim) print(f"{label:<13} depth at 10s/20s/30s = {rows[9]}/{rows[19]}/{rows[29]}, " f"dropped {dropped}, newest item waits {wait:.1f}s")

The unbounded queue reaches 600 messages after 30 s and the newest item waits 6 s, growing without limit. The bounded queue holds at 200, drops 400 messages (the caller sees a fast "try later"), and latency stays at 2 s. Failing fast at the edge is kinder than accepting work you cannot finish. The relation used above, Little's law (average items in the system = arrival rate x average time in the system), is worth memorizing: it links queue depth, throughput and latency.

1.8 — Rate Limiting: Protecting the System From Its Own Callers

The problem

Asha's public API has no limits. One customer writes a script with a bug that calls the search endpoint in a tight loop: 4,000 requests per second from a single API key. The database saturates and every other customer sees errors. Nothing was "attacked"; the system was simply too polite to say no. Rate limiting caps how much any one caller can take, so a single noisy neighbor cannot starve everyone else.

The idea: a budget per caller

Give every caller (per user, API key, or IP address) a budget and refuse work beyond it. The refusal should be fast, cheap and clear: HTTP status 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header telling the caller when to come back. Think of a bus with a fixed number of seats: once full, the driver says "next bus" instead of squeezing in more people until the axle breaks.

Limits serve three separate goals, and it helps to name which one you have: fairness between customers, protection of a fragile dependency, and cost control for expensive endpoints (an LLM call is priced very differently from a health check).

The token bucket is the workhorse. The bucket holds up to capacity tokens and refills at rate tokens per second. Each request removes one token; if the bucket is empty the request is denied. The capacity is the burst allowance; the refill rate is the sustained rate. That matches how real clients behave: quiet, then a short burst, then quiet.

The refill rate is the real limit; the bucket only shapes bursts.

Why fixed windows are leaky

Fixed windows are the simplest to build (one counter per window) and also the easiest to game. If the limit is 5 requests per 10 seconds, a caller sends five at second 9 and five more at second 10. Each window sees five, all ten pass, yet ten requests arrived within a single second.

The fixed-window boundary burst
LIMIT, WINDOW = 5, 10 reqs = [9.0] * 5 + [10.0] * 5 # five just before the boundary, five just after def fixed_window(reqs): counts, ok = {}, 0 for t in reqs: w = int(t // WINDOW) if counts.get(w, 0) < LIMIT: counts[w] = counts.get(w, 0) + 1; ok += 1 return ok def sliding_log(reqs): log, ok = [], 0 for t in reqs: log = [x for x in log if x > t - WINDOW] if len(log) < LIMIT: log.append(t); ok += 1 return ok print("fixed window allowed :", fixed_window(reqs), "of", len(reqs), "(limit is 5 per 10 s)") print("sliding log allowed :", sliding_log(reqs), "of", len(reqs))
fixed window allowed : 10 of 10 (limit is 5 per 10 s) sliding log allowed : 5 of 10

Choose fixed windows when approximate is fine and you want the cheapest possible check. Choose sliding or token bucket when the limit protects something fragile.

Where the limiter lives

A limiter that keeps its counters in one process only works with one process. With ten API servers behind a load balancer each server sees a tenth of the traffic and lets ten times too much through. Options: keep counters in a shared fast store (an in-memory database with atomic operations), route each caller consistently to the same limiter node (consistent hashing), or accept approximate limits with small local buckets that sync periodically. The rate limiter case study later builds this.

Omitting Retry-After so clients guess and hammer. Failing closed on limiter outage (limiter down means API down): decide deliberately, usually fail open with a coarse local limit. Not limiting expensive endpoints more tightly than cheap ones.

Recap
  • A rate limiter gives each caller a budget and rejects the excess fast with 429 and Retry-After.
  • Token bucket: capacity is the burst allowance, refill rate is the sustained rate.
  • Fixed windows can let through 2x the limit at a boundary; sliding windows and token buckets avoid that.
  • Counters must be shared (or consistently routed) across servers, and you must decide how the limiter fails.
1.9 — Load Balancing: Spreading Work and Surviving Dead Servers

The problem

Ben runs one application server at 60% CPU on a normal day. Sales day doubles traffic and the machine falls over; then, at 3 a.m., the machine itself dies and the site is down until someone wakes up. One server is both a capacity ceiling and a single point of failure. Two servers fix nothing unless something decides which one gets each request.

The idea: a front door that picks a healthy server

A load balancer (LB) sits in front of a pool of identical servers, accepts each request, and forwards it to one server. It removes a dead server from rotation using health checks. Think of a supermarket with several checkouts and a person at the front pointing shoppers to a free till.

There are two levels. A layer-4 LB forwards TCP connections by address and port: very fast, blind to the content. A layer-7 LB understands HTTP: it can route by path or host header (/api to one pool, /static to another), terminate TLS, add headers, and retry idempotent requests. Most systems use L7 at the edge and sometimes L4 in front of it.

Round robin versus smarter routing
def workload(n, seed=11): s, out = seed, [] for _ in range(n): s = (s * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2**31 r = s / 2**31 out.append(30 if r > 0.97 else 1 + int(r * 4)) # 3% heavy requests return out def run(policy, n=5000, servers=4): backlog, waits, rr, s = [0.0] * servers, [], 0, 99 for w in workload(n): if policy == "round_robin": i = rr % servers; rr += 1 elif policy == "least_loaded": i = min(range(servers), key=lambda j: backlog[j]) else: # power of two choices s = (s * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2**31; a = s % servers s = (s * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2**31; b = s % servers i = a if backlog[a] <= backlog[b] else b waits.append(backlog[i]); backlog[i] += w backlog = [max(0.0, x - 1.0) for x in backlog] # each server does 1 unit/tick waits.sort() return sum(waits) / n, waits[int(n * 0.99)] for p in ("round_robin", "power_of_two", "least_loaded"): mean, p99 = run(p) print(f"{p:<13} mean wait {mean:6.2f} ticks p99 wait {p99:6.1f} ticks")

Reading it. Every request waits for whatever is already queued on its chosen server. Round robin ignores queues: a heavy request arriving on a server that just took another heavy request sits behind 60 units of work, giving a mean wait of 10.5 ticks and a p99 of 77. Picking the better of just two random servers halves the mean wait (5.2) and cuts p99 to 51. Always choosing the least loaded is best here (mean 1.5, p99 20) but assumes exact, instantaneous knowledge of every queue; with many balancers and delayed information it can send everyone to the same "emptiest" server at once (a herd). Power of two choices is popular because it gets most of the benefit with no coordination.

Health, draining and stickiness

- Health checks can be shallow (process is up) or deep (can reach the database). Deep checks catch more, but a shared dependency blip can make the LB pull every server out at once. Prefer a shallow liveness check for removal plus a separate readiness signal. - Connection draining: when you deploy or scale in, stop sending new requests to a server but let in-flight ones finish. Skipping this drops customer requests on every deploy. - Sticky sessions pin a user to one server. They are convenient when session state lives in memory but they make load uneven and lose the session when that server dies. Better: keep the session in a shared store or in a signed token, and make servers stateless. - Slow starts and warm-up: a new server with a cold cache needs a ramp, or it is crushed the moment it joins.

⚠️
Common mistakesTreating the LB as infinitely reliable when it is one box. Round robin in front of requests with wildly different costs. Health checks that hit the database, so a database blip empties the pool. Sticky sessions used to avoid fixing server state. Forgetting that balancing hides a slow server: watch per-server latency, not only the fleet average.
Check yourself: One of four servers becomes twice as slow because of a noisy disk. Which routing policy notices, and which does not?
Least connections or power of two choices notice, because queues on the slow server grow and they route away from it. Plain round robin keeps sending it a quarter of the traffic and its latency drags the tail.
Recap
  • An LB gives capacity and failover: many servers behind one address, unhealthy ones removed.
  • L4 forwards connections; L7 understands HTTP and can route by path.
  • Round robin ignores load; power of two choices is a cheap, strong default; least loaded needs accurate information.
  • Prefer stateless servers, connection draining, and shallow health checks; make the LB itself redundant.
1.10 — API Design and Pagination: Contracts That Survive Retries and Growth

The problem

Chen's "list orders" endpoint returns everything. It worked with 200 orders. At 400,000 orders one request ships 90 MB and times out. Then the team "fixes" it with ?page=3, and users start reporting duplicated and missing rows. Separately, a mobile client on a bad network retries POST /orders and the customer is charged twice. These are all API-contract problems: the design did not say what happens at scale or on retry.

The idea: design the contract for the bad day

A good API is a small set of predictable operations, with explicit behavior for growth (pagination), duplicates (idempotency), change (versioning) and failure (a consistent error model). Think of it as the label on a shipping crate: whoever handles it in the future must be able to act correctly by reading the label alone.

Pagination: offset versus cursor

Offset pagination (?offset=20&limit=10) is simple, but it counts positions, and positions move. If new items arrive at the top while a reader moves from page 1 to page 2, everything shifts down and page 2 repeats items from page 1. It also gets slower with depth, because the database must walk past all skipped rows. Cursor (keyset) pagination says "give me items after this id" and the cursor is an anchor to a specific item rather than a position.
Offset drift versus a stable cursor
feed = sorted(range(1, 21), reverse=True) # ids 20..1, newest first def offset_page(data, offset, limit): return data[offset:offset + limit] def cursor_page(data, after_id, limit): rows = [x for x in data if after_id is None or x < after_id] page = rows[:limit] return page, (page[-1] if len(page) == limit else None) p1 = offset_page(feed, 0, 5) c1, cur = cursor_page(feed, None, 5) # the cursor is the last id seen feed = [21, 22] + feed # two new items arrive while the user reads p2_offset = offset_page(feed, 5, 5) c2, _ = cursor_page(feed, cur, 5) print("page 1 :", p1) print("page 2 (offset) :", p2_offset, "<- repeats", sorted(set(p1) & set(p2_offset))) print("page 2 (cursor) :", c2, "<- repeats", sorted(set(c1) & set(c2)))
page 1 : [20, 19, 18, 17, 16] page 2 (offset) : [17, 16, 15, 14, 13] <- repeats [16, 17] page 2 (cursor) : [15, 14, 13, 12, 11] <- repeats []
Trace. Both readers see ids 20 to 16 first. Two new items (21, 22) are inserted. Offset page 2 counts five positions from the top again, but the list is now two items longer at the front, so items 17 and 16 reappear. The cursor version asks for "ids below 16", so it returns 15 to 11 with no repeats regardless of what was inserted. The price: cursors cannot jump to "page 37", and the sort key must be unique and indexed (usually (created_at, id)). For search results where users jump around, offset with a small maximum depth is acceptable. For feeds, exports and anything large, use cursors and return an opaque next_cursor token.

Idempotency keys for writes

GET, PUT and DELETE are naturally safe to repeat. POST is not: repeating "create order" creates two. The standard fix is an idempotency key: the client generates a unique id for the intent and sends it in a header; the server stores the first result under that key and replays it for repeats.

Idempotency key: same request twice, one order
store = {} # idempotency key -> stored result def create_order(idem_key, payload): if idem_key in store: return store[idem_key], 200 # replay: same answer, no new order order = {"id": len(store) + 1, **payload} store[idem_key] = order return order, 201 for key, body in [("k1", {"item": "pen"}), ("k1", {"item": "pen"}), ("k2", {"item": "ink"})]: print(create_order(key, body)) print("orders created:", len(store))

The second call with k1 returns the same order with status 200 and does not create anything: two distinct intents (k1, k2), two orders. In real systems the key and the result are stored in the same transaction as the order, keys expire after a day or so, and a repeated key with a different body is rejected.

1.11 — Observability: Knowing What the System Is Doing

The problem

At 02:10 the pager fires: "checkout is slow". Dana opens the dashboard: average latency looks normal, CPU is fine, and the error rate is flat. Yet customers are complaining. Without the right measurements, she spends four hours guessing. A system you cannot see into is a system you cannot operate; observability is the ability to answer new questions about what the system is doing from the data it emits, without shipping new code.

The idea: three signals and a few good numbers

The three classic signals are metrics (numbers over time: cheap, good for alerting), logs (events with details: good for "what happened to this request"), and traces (the path of one request through many services with timings: good for "where did the time go"). Metrics tell you that something is wrong, traces tell you where, logs tell you why. A correlation id (request id) passed through every service and written into every log line ties them together.

For any service, start with the RED method: Rate (requests per second), Errors (failures per second or fraction), Duration (latency distribution). For resources (CPU, disk, queues) use USE: Utilization, Saturation (queue length), Errors.

Averages lie; percentiles tell the truth

Latency is not one number but a distribution with a long tail. The mean is dragged around by the fast majority and hides the slow minority. The p99 is the value below which 99% of requests fall: one in a hundred is slower. Because a page load often makes dozens of calls, most users hit the tail somewhere.

Mean hides the tail

The mean is 79 ms, which looks healthy. The median (p50) is 60 ms and p95 is 78 ms. Only p99 reveals that one request in a hundred takes two full seconds. Burn 1x uses the whole budget in 30 days; burn 14x uses it in about two days.

1.12 — Failure Modes: Timeouts, Retries, Circuit Breakers, Graceful Degradation

The problem

Eli's checkout calls a recommendations service to decorate the cart page. One afternoon recommendations slows from 20 ms to 30 seconds. Checkout threads wait for it, the thread pool fills, and now checkout itself is down, even though it did not need recommendations to take payment. Clients time out and retry, tripling the load on the struggling service. A slow dependency is more dangerous than a dead one, and retries turn a blip into an outage.

The idea: assume every call can fail, and decide what happens next

Every network call has four possible outcomes: fast success, fast failure, slow success, and never returning. The slow ones are the killers. A senior design answers for each dependency: how long do we wait, do we retry, when do we stop trying, and what do we show instead?

  • Timeouts on every call, shorter than the caller's own deadline. No timeout means a stuck dependency freezes you.
  • Retries only for idempotent operations and transient errors, with exponential backoff and jitter, and a small retry budget.
  • Circuit breaker: after repeated failures, stop calling for a cooldown and fail fast, then probe with one request.
  • Bulkheads: separate thread or connection pools per dependency so one slow service cannot eat everything.
  • Graceful degradation: choose a fallback (cached value, default, hide the feature) for non-essential dependencies.

Retries with backoff and jitter

Backoff waits longer after each failure (0.1 s, 0.2 s, 0.4 s ...). Jitter randomizes each wait so a thousand clients that failed together do not all retry together.

Bounded retries: ok, gave up, and the amplification
def call_with_retries(fail_times, max_retries, base=0.1, cap=2.0, jitter=True): waited, seed = 0.0, 3 for attempt in range(max_retries + 1): if attempt >= fail_times: return f"ok after {attempt} retries, waited {waited:.2f}s" if attempt == max_retries: return f"gave up after {attempt} retries, waited {waited:.2f}s" delay = min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt) if jitter: seed = (seed * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2**31 delay = delay * (0.5 + seed / 2**31 / 2) # 50% to 100% of the delay waited += delay print(call_with_retries(2, 4)) print(call_with_retries(9, 4)) n, per = 1000, 3 # 1000 clients, 3 retries each print("load on a struggling service: normal", n, "req/s ->", n * (1 + per), "req/s with naive retries")
ok after 2 retries, waited 0.20s gave up after 4 retries, waited 0.96s load on a struggling service: normal 1000 req/s -> 4000 req/s with naive retries

A transient failure clears after two retries in 0.2 s; a permanent one is abandoned after four (the wait is bounded). The last line is the danger: when every layer retries three times, a service that is already sick receives four times its normal load. With three layers each retrying three times the multiplier is 4x4x4 = 64. Cap total retries with a retry budget (for example: retries may be at most 10% of requests) and retry at only one layer.

The circuit breaker

The breaker has three states: closed (calls flow), open (calls fail instantly; the sick service gets a rest), half-open (after the cooldown, let one probe through; success closes the circuit, failure reopens it). Think of the fuse box in a house: when something is wrong the switch flips so the whole house does not burn.

A circuit breaker in twenty lines
class Breaker: def __init__(self, threshold=3, cooldown=5): self.threshold, self.cooldown = threshold, cooldown self.fails, self.opened_at, self.state = 0, None, "closed" def call(self, now, service_ok): if self.state == "open": if now - self.opened_at < self.cooldown: return "fast-fail" # do not even try self.state = "half-open" # allow one probe if service_ok: self.fails, self.state = 0, "closed" return "ok" self.fails += 1 if self.state == "half-open" or self.fails >= self.threshold: self.state, self.opened_at = "open", now return "error" b = Breaker() outage = range(2, 12) # service down for t=2..11 log = [(t, b.call(t, t not in outage)) for t in range(16)] print(" ".join(f"{t}:{r}" for t, r in log)) print("calls that reached the sick service:", sum(r == "error" for _, r in log))

Trace. The service breaks at t=2. Three consecutive errors (t=2, 3, 4) open the circuit. During the cooldown (t=5 to 8) every call fails fast without touching the service. Degrade, do not collapse

When a dependency is optional, decide the fallback in advance. Recommendations down: show popular items from cache. Search down: allow browsing by category. Payment provider slow: queue the order as "pending". The customer sees a slightly worse product instead of an error page. The same idea applies to load: under overload shed low-priority work first (analytics, prefetching) to keep checkout alive.

Common mistakesNo timeout (the default in many libraries is "wait forever"). Retrying non-idempotent calls. Retrying at every layer. Check yourself: Service A (timeout 2 s) calls B, which calls C with a 3 s timeout. What is wrong?

Recap
  • Slow dependencies are more dangerous than dead ones; put a timeout on every call.
  • Retry only idempotent, transient failures with backoff, jitter, and a budget; beware multiplied retries across layers.
  • A circuit breaker fails fast during outages and probes to recover; bulkheads limit blast radius.
  • Decide fallbacks in advance: degrade optional features, shed low-priority work.

Requirements

- Functional: users follow other users, publish posts, and open a home feed showing recent posts from people they follow, newest first. - Non-functional: opening the feed feels instant (p99 under 300 ms); a new post shows up for followers within about 10 seconds; the feed may be slightly stale but must never show someone else's private posts. - Assumptions: 50 million daily active users; average person follows 200 accounts; a tiny number of accounts have millions of followers.

Design

Hybrid feed pipeline

Store only post ids in each feed list, and fetch the bodies afterward ("hydration"), so deleting or editing a post affects one place. Bottlenecks and failure modes

- Fan-out lag: a backed-up queue means posts appear late. Common mistakesChoosing push or pull as a religion. Storing whole post bodies in every follower's list (huge duplication and impossible deletion). Fanning out to accounts that never log in. Offset pagination on a live feed. Forgetting the unfollow and block paths, which must remove or filter content quickly.
Check yourself: A regular user with 500 followers suddenly goes viral and gains 5 million followers in an hour. What does the hybrid design need to handle this?
A threshold check that switches the author from push to pull once follower count crosses the limit, plus a way to stop treating already-pushed posts as authoritative (readers merge the celebrity's recent posts at read time). Otherwise the next post triggers a five-million-write storm.
Recap
  • The core decision is when to build the feed: on write (push), on read (pull), or both (hybrid).
  • Push is best for many small authors; pull for authors with huge audiences; the threshold is data driven.
  • Store ids in feed lists and hydrate from cache; page by cursor.
  • Plan for lag, hot keys, cache loss and privacy checks at read time.

The deep dive: atomicity

The tempting implementation is: read the counter, decide, write the new counter. With many servers this is a race: two servers read the same value, both decide "allowed", both write back. The check-and-update must be one atomic operation on the node that owns the bucket (a single-threaded command, a Lua script, a compare-and-swap loop). Watch what non-atomic does to a limit of 5.

1.17 — Case Study 5: An ML Model-Serving Platformcase study

Your company wants many teams to deploy models behind an API: a ranking model, a fraud model, an embedding model. Each team should be able to ship a new version without breaking others. This is a system-design problem with a twist: the expensive resource is an accelerator (GPU or similar) that is most efficient when it processes many inputs together.

Requirements

- Functional: register a model version; serve predictions over an API; route traffic between versions (canary, rollback); per-team quotas. - Non-functional: p99 latency under 150 ms for online models; 99.9% availability; cost efficiency (accelerators are expensive); any model version can be rolled back within minutes; every prediction attributable to a model version for debugging. - Assumptions: one flagship model at 3,000 requests per second at peak; a batch of 8 takes 36 ms on one replica, and a batch of 1 takes 22 ms (fixed launch cost plus a per-item cost).

The deep dive: dynamic batching

Running one request at a time wastes the accelerator: most of the 22 ms is fixed overhead. Dynamic batching waits a short time to collect several requests and runs them together. It trades a little latency (waiting for company) for a lot of throughput. Simulate a replica receiving 150 requests per second, with various limits on batch size and maximum wait.

Dynamic batching: throughput versus latency
def batch_time(b): return 20 + 2 * b # ms: fixed launch cost + per-item cost def simulate(rate_per_s, max_batch, max_wait_ms, n=3000): arrivals = [i * 1000 / rate_per_s for i in range(n)] free_at, lat, i, batches = 0.0, [], 0, 0 while i < n: start = max(free_at, arrivals[i]) limit = max(start, arrivals[i] + max_wait_ms) # wait for company, but not forever j = i + 1 while j < n and j - i < max_batch and arrivals[j] <= limit: j += 1 full = j - i == max_batch dispatch = max(start, arrivals[j - 1]) if full else limit done = dispatch + batch_time(j - i) lat += [done - a for a in arrivals[i:j]] free_at, i, batches = done, j, batches + 1 lat.sort() return n / batches, sum(lat) / n, lat[int(.99 * n)] for mb, mw in ((1, 0), (4, 5), (8, 10), (16, 20), (32, 50)): avg_b, mean, p99 = simulate(150, mb, mw) print(f"max_batch {mb:>2} wait<={mw:>2} ms -> avg batch {avg_b:5.1f}, mean {mean:8.1f} ms, p99 {p99:8.1f} ms")
max_batch 1 wait<= 0 ms -> avg batch 1.0, mean 23014.3 ms, p99 45562.0 ms max_batch 4 wait<= 5 ms -> avg batch 4.0, mean 537.3 ms, p99 1028.3 ms max_batch 8 wait<=10 ms -> avg batch 4.3, mean 43.0 ms, p99 57.3 ms max_batch 16 wait<=20 ms -> avg batch 4.3, mean 43.0 ms, p99 57.3 ms max_batch 32 wait<=50 ms -> avg batch 8.0, mean 62.7 ms, p99 86.0 ms
Reading it. With no batching (max_batch 1) one replica can finish only 45 requests per second, so at 150 per second the queue grows without bound: mean latency is 23 seconds. A limit of 4 is still slightly overloaded (mean 537 ms). At max_batch 8 the replica keeps up: mean 43 ms, p99 57 ms, and batches average 4.3 because requests arrive as fast as they can be served. Raising the limits further (16, then 32 with a 50 ms wait) does not help at this load: the larger wait simply adds latency, 63 ms mean and 86 ms p99. Batching is a trade-off you tune with the arrival rate, not "bigger is better", and the maximum wait must fit inside your latency budget.

Estimates: replicas and cost

Throughput per replica is batch / batch_time. Size for target utilisation (60%) so bursts and a lost replica do not tip you over.

Replicas and cost per batch size
import math def batch_time(b): return 20 + 2 * b # ms per batch on one replica peak_qps, target_util, price_per_hour = 3000, 0.6, 2.5 for b in (1, 8, 32): per_replica = b / batch_time(b) * 1000 # requests per second at full batches replicas = math.ceil(peak_qps / (per_replica * target_util)) print(f"batch {b:>2}: {per_replica:6.0f} req/s per replica -> {replicas:>3} replicas, ${replicas * price_per_hour * 24:,.0f}/day")

The estimates also show why a serving platform is cost-driven: the model, not the web server, is the bill.

Canary routing must be sticky per user or request key, otherwise a user flip-flops between model versions. Hash the user id into a bucket from 0 to 99 and send buckets below the canary percentage to the new version. Because the bucket is a pure function of the id, raising the percentage only adds users; nobody leaves the canary.
Sticky canary by hashing
import hashlib def bucket(user_id, salt="m2"): return int(hashlib.md5(f"{salt}:{user_id}".encode()).hexdigest()[:8], 16) % 100 def route(user_id, canary_pct): return "v2" if bucket(user_id) < canary_pct else "v1" users = range(10_000) for pct in (0, 5, 25, 100): n2 = sum(route(u, pct) == "v2" for u in users) print(f"canary {pct:>3}%: {n2:>5} of 10000 users on v2") s5 = {u for u in users if route(u, 5) == "v2"} s25 = {u for u in users if route(u, 25) == "v2"} print("everyone on v2 at 5% is still on v2 at 25%:", s5 <= s25)

1.18 — Thinking Like a Senior: Trade-offs, Deep Dives and Design Reviews

The problem

Two engineers propose designs for the same feature. Both are technically fine. One says: "We will use Redis and Kafka." The other says: "Reads are 100 times writes, so we cache; the cost is up to 60 seconds of staleness, which product accepted; if the cache dies, the database can carry 30% of traffic, so we shed the rest; here is how we will know." The second engineer gets the project, and the first will spend months defending choices nobody understood. Seniority shows up as reasoning made visible.

The idea: every choice is a trade, and you name the price

There is no best design, only the best design for these numbers and constraints. A senior engineer states, for each major decision, four things: the options considered, the deciding factor, what is given up, and the trigger that would change the decision ("if traffic exceeds 20,000 writes per second, we revisit sharding"). Writing this down is a decision record, and it is the most valuable paragraph in a design doc because it survives after the people leave.

A simple tool for making a trade-off explicit is a weighted decision matrix. It does not compute the truth; it forces you to state your weights, which is where teams actually disagree.

A weighted decision matrix
weights = {"read latency": 5, "write scalability": 2, "operational simplicity": 4, "cost": 3} options = { # score 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) per criterion "single DB + cache": {"read latency": 4, "write scalability": 2, "operational simplicity": 5, "cost": 5}, "sharded DB": {"read latency": 4, "write scalability": 5, "operational simplicity": 2, "cost": 2}, "wide-column store": {"read latency": 3, "write scalability": 5, "operational simplicity": 3, "cost": 3}, } def total(scores): return sum(weights[c] * scores[c] for c in weights) for name, scores in sorted(options.items(), key=lambda kv: -total(kv[1])): print(f"{name:<20} {total(scores):>3}") weights["write scalability"] = 10 # what if writes will grow 100x? print("after raising the write weight:", max(options, key=lambda n: total(options[n])))
single DB + cache 59 wide-column store 46 sharded DB 44 after raising the write weight: wide-column store
Reading it. With today's weights the boring option (one database plus a cache) wins, 59 to 46 to 44. The exact numbers matter less than the last line: raise one weight (writes will grow a hundred times) and the winner becomes the wide-column store. The decision depended on one assumption. That is the useful output: it shows which assumption the decision depends on, so you can go and measure it.

How to run the deep dive

Pick the component whose failure or scale would hurt most and go one level down: the schema and its indexes, the algorithm, the exact failure behavior. Typical deep dives are the id generator in a shortener, the fan-out in a feed, the atomic check in a limiter, the ordering in a chat, the batcher in a serving platform. A good deep dive names the data structure, gives a number, and says what happens when it fails.

A review checklist you can run on any design

  • Numbers: are QPS, storage and latency budgets written down, with the arithmetic visible?
  • Single points of failure: for every box, what happens when it dies? Is the answer written?
  • State: where does each piece of state live, and who is the source of truth?
  • Consistency: which reads may be stale, for how long, and does the product agree?
  • Retries: which operations are idempotent? What happens on a duplicate or an out-of-order message?
  • Overload: what is shed first, and how do callers learn to back off?
  • Operations: what will you page on, how will you roll back, and how does data migrate?
  • Cost and team: can the team of the size you have actually run this at 3 a.m.?

Question 1 of 5
A read-heavy service takes 5,000 requests per second and the database can serve 600. A cache raises the hit rate from 90% to 99%. Predict what happens to the load on the database.
Why: Misses go to the database: 10% of 5,000 is 500 per second, 1% is 50. Small gains near the top of the hit-rate curve remove most of the remaining load, which is why a cache is mainly a shield for the database.
Question 2 of 5
A queue delivers at-least-once. A consumer that credits an account per message sometimes double-credits after a lost acknowledgement. What is the best fix?
Why: At-most-once trades duplicates for lost credits, which is worse. Faster consumers do not remove lost acks, and infinite instant retries cause storms. Exactly-once effects come from at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing.
Question 3 of 5
You shard 10,000 keys across 4 servers with hash(key) % N, then add a fifth server. Compared with a consistent hash ring with virtual nodes, predict the difference in how many keys move.
Why: Changing N in hash-mod-N changes the home of almost every key (about 4 out of 5). On the ring the new node only takes the arcs just before its points, close to the ideal one fifth.
Question 4 of 5
Your service needs three components in series (app, database, payment provider), each with 99.9% availability. A colleague wants to promise customers 99.95%. What is the right judgement?
Why: Serial dependencies multiply: 0.999 x 0.999 x 0.999 is about 0.997, roughly 130 minutes of downtime a month. To promise more you must add independent redundancy, make a dependency optional, or lower the promise.
Question 5 of 5
A public API runs on 10 servers. Each server enforces a limit of 100 requests per minute per customer using its own in-memory counter. What can one customer actually achieve, and what is the fix?
Why: Each server sees only its own slice, so the effective limit is roughly servers times the local limit. Shared counters (with atomic check-and-take) or consistent routing by customer id restore a real global limit.
